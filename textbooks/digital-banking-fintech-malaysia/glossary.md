# Glossary

All terms are defined as they appear in the chapters; the number in brackets is the chapter where the term is first defined.

**51% attack** [4]: an attack in which one actor controls the majority of a network's mining power or stake, enabling it to rewrite recent history and double-spend.

**Accessibility** [6]: The extent to which a service can be understood and used by people with different abilities, devices, connectivity and levels of digital literacy.

**Account takeover (ATO)** [8]: unauthorised access in which a criminal gains control of a genuine customer's account using stolen credentials.

**Adoption readiness** [6]: An assessment of whether a technology, institution, customer need and supporting safeguards are mature enough for a controlled pilot or wider use.

**Algorithmic bias** [8]: systematic error in an algorithm's outputs that produces unfair outcomes for particular groups of people.

**Algorithmic trading** [5]: the use of computer programs to generate and submit buy or sell orders automatically according to defined rules, with little or no human intervention at execution.

**Altcoin** [4]: any cryptocurrency other than bitcoin, spanning platform, payment, utility, governance, meme and stablecoin categories.

**Alternative data** [3]: non-traditional digital information — such as bank transaction flows, e-payment records and online sales — used to assess creditworthiness when formal records are thin.

**Application programming interface (API)** [2]: a standardised set of rules allowing one software system to request data or services from another in a controlled, machine-readable way.

**Arbitrage** [5]: the simultaneous purchase and sale of the same or economically equivalent assets in different markets or forms to profit from a price discrepancy.

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** [7]: the field of building computer systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising images and making decisions.

**Augmented reality (AR)** [6]: Digital information layered onto a view of the physical environment, commonly through a phone or wearable display.

**Authorised push payment (APP) scam** [8]: a fraud in which the victim is deceived into personally authorising a transfer to the criminal's account.

**Backtesting** [5]: simulating a trading strategy on historical market data to estimate how it would have performed before risking real capital.

**BigTech** [1]: Large technology companies with vast user bases and data advantages that expand into financial services such as payments, lending and banking.

**Bitcoin** [4]: the first decentralised cryptocurrency, proposed by Satoshi Nakamoto in 2008 and launched in 2009, enabling peer-to-peer value transfer without intermediaries.

**Black-box problem** [7]: the inability of humans to understand or explain precisely why a complex ML model produced a particular decision.

**Block** [4]: a bundle of validated transactions together with a header containing metadata such as the hash, previous-hash, timestamp, nonce and Merkle root.

**Blockchain** [4]: a type of distributed ledger in which validated transactions are grouped into blocks that are cryptographically linked in chronological order.

**Business continuity plan (BCP)** [8]: a documented, tested strategy for maintaining or rapidly recovering critical functions during a disruption.

**Business model** [1]: The plan describing how a firm creates, delivers and captures value, including its sources of revenue and cost structure.

**Central bank digital currency (CBDC)** [2]: a digital form of central-bank money; a direct liability of the central bank, denominated in the national unit of account, in wholesale or retail form.

**Chatbot** [7]: an AI system that converses with customers in natural language to answer queries and perform simple transactions.

**Concentration risk** [8]: systemic exposure created when many institutions depend on a small number of third-party providers, such as major cloud platforms.

**Consensus mechanism** [4]: the rules and incentives by which nodes in a distributed network agree on valid transactions and the official state of the ledger.

**Credit scoring** [3]: the use of statistical models to estimate the probability that a borrower will repay a debt, typically summarised as a score or risk grade.

**Crowdfunding** [3]: raising small amounts of money from a large number of people through an online platform to fund a project, cause or business.

**Cryptographic agility** [6]: The ability to replace or update cryptographic algorithms and keys without disrupting essential systems.

**Customer due diligence (CDD)** [2]: the process by which a financial institution identifies and verifies a customer, understands the purpose of the relationship, and monitors transactions accordingly.

**Data breach** [8]: unauthorised access to or disclosure of personal or financial data, often arming future scams.

**Data minimisation** [6]: Collecting and retaining only the personal data necessary for a stated and legitimate purpose.

**Decentralised finance (DeFi)** [4]: an ecosystem of blockchain-based applications that provide lending, trading and other financial services through smart contracts without intermediaries.

**Deep learning** [7]: a subset of ML using multi-layered neural networks to learn complex patterns from large volumes of data.

**Default risk** [3]: the risk that a borrower fails to make required payments, causing lenders or investors to lose returns, principal or both.

**Digital asset exchange (DAX)** [1]: A platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia on which digital assets, prescribed as securities under Malaysian law, may be traded.

**Digital bank** [2]: a licensed bank that delivers all services through digital channels, operating without any physical branch network.

**Digital banking** [1]: The delivery of licensed banking services wholly or almost wholly through digital or electronic means, with remote onboarding and no reliance on branches.

**Digital financing** [3]: the provision of financial services, especially credit and capital raising, through digital technologies and platforms rather than traditional branch-based processes.

**Digital investment management (DIM)** [1]: SC-licensed automated investment services — robo-advisory — covering risk profiling, suitability assessment, asset allocation and rebalancing with minimal human supervision.

**Dilution** [3]: the reduction in existing shareholders' ownership percentage when new shares are issued to new investors, even though their share count is unchanged.

**Direct market access (DMA)** [5]: an arrangement allowing a broker's client to transmit orders electronically through the broker's infrastructure directly into an exchange's order book, subject to pre-trade controls.

**Distributed ledger technology (DLT)** [4]: a digital record-keeping system in which a ledger is shared, replicated and synchronised across multiple network nodes without a single central authority.

**Double-spending problem** [4]: the risk that digital money, being copyable, is spent more than once; blockchains solve it without a central ledger-keeper.

**DuitNow** [2]: Malaysia's instant account-to-account transfer service (2018), addressed to proxies such as a mobile or MyKad number instead of a bank account number.

**DuitNow QR** [2]: Malaysia's national interoperable QR payment standard (2019); one merchant QR code can be scanned by any participating banking app or e-wallet.

**E-wallet** [1]: A smartphone application that stores e-money and enables payments, transfers and often additional services such as investments or insurance.

**Effective annual cost** [3]: the total cost of financing, including returns and all fees, expressed as an annual rate on the net funds actually received by the borrower.

**Electronic know-your-customer (e-KYC)** [1]: Regulator-approved digital processes for verifying a customer's identity remotely, enabling non-face-to-face account opening.

**Electronic money (e-money)** [1]: Digitally stored monetary value issued under a BNM licence that holders can use to pay merchants or transfer to other users.

**Embedded finance** [1]: The integration of financial services such as payments, lending or insurance directly into non-financial apps and platforms at the point of need.

**Environmental, social and governance (ESG)** [9]: the three-pillar framework for assessing the sustainability and ethical impact of a company or investment.

**Equity crowdfunding (ECF)** [1]: The raising of equity capital by companies from many investors through an online platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia.

**Exchange-traded fund (ETF)** [5]: a listed, usually passively managed fund that tracks an index and trades intraday on an exchange like an ordinary share.

**Explainability** [8]: the degree to which a human can understand and articulate how a model reached a particular decision.

**FEAT principles** [7]: MAS's 2018 benchmark requiring AI and data analytics in finance to be Fair, Ethical, Accountable and Transparent.

**Financial inclusion** [1]: Access of individuals and businesses to useful, affordable financial products and services — payments, savings, credit and insurance — delivered responsibly and sustainably.

**Financial technology (FinTech)** [1]: Digital innovations and technology-enabled business model innovations in the financial sector; used for both the industry of firms and the underlying technologies.

**Flash crash** [5]: an extremely rapid, deep and largely self-reversing fall in market prices driven by interacting automated trading systems, exemplified by the 2010 US Flash Crash.

**Fraud detection** [7]: the use of ML models to score transactions for suspiciousness in real time based on learned fraud patterns.

**Freemium** [1]: A business model offering basic services free to build a large user base, then charging for premium features or cross-selling other products.

**Full node** [4]: a node that downloads the entire blockchain and independently verifies every transaction and block against the consensus rules.

**Gas** [4]: the unit measuring computational work on Ethereum, paid in ether, which compensates validators and deters spam.

**Generative AI** [7]: AI models that create new content — text, images, code or audio — in response to prompts.

**Gharar** [9]: excessive uncertainty or ambiguity in a contract's subject matter, price or obligations, which renders the contract invalid under Shariah.

**Green sukuk** [9]: a sukuk whose proceeds are used exclusively to finance environmentally beneficial projects, passing both Shariah and environmental review.

**Greenwashing** [9]: exaggerating or misrepresenting the environmental or social benefits of a financial product or activity to attract capital.

**Halving** [4]: the programmed reduction, roughly every four years, of Bitcoin's block reward by half; the April 2024 halving cut it to 3.125 BTC.

**Hash** [4]: the fixed-length output of a one-way hash function; a digital fingerprint that uniquely represents any input and changes completely if the input changes.

**High-frequency trading (HFT)** [5]: a specialised form of algorithmic trading using low-latency technology to trade very large volumes over extremely short holding periods, often milliseconds.

**Human-in-the-loop (HITL)** [7]: a safeguard in which a human reviews, can override and remains accountable for AI recommendations in high-stakes decisions.

**Immersive technology** [6]: An interface that places a user in a computer-generated environment or overlays digital information on the physical environment.

**Information asymmetry** [3]: a situation in which one party to a transaction holds more or better information than the other, complicating lending and investment decisions.

**Initial coin offering (ICO)** [4]: a largely unregulated public sale of newly created tokens to raise funds, notorious for failures and fraud during 2017–2018.

**Interchange fee** [1]: The portion of a card transaction fee paid by the merchant's bank to the cardholder's issuing bank, forming a key revenue source for card issuers.

**Internet of Things (IoT)** [6]: Connected physical devices that collect or exchange data through sensors, software and networks.

**Investment note** [3]: a security issued through a registered P2P platform evidencing the issuer's obligation to repay investors principal plus agreed returns over a fixed tenor.

**Islamic digital finance** [9]: the use of financial technologies to deliver products and services that comply with Shariah principles in both contract and operation.

**Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 (IFSA)** [9]: the Malaysian statute regulating Islamic financial institutions and imposing a statutory duty of continuous Shariah compliance.

**Issuer** [3]: the business that raises funds on a P2P or ECF platform by issuing investment notes or shares, and bears the obligation to repay or to share ownership.

**Kill-switch** [8]: a self-service bank feature allowing customers to freeze their own accounts and cards immediately upon suspecting compromise.

**Light node** [4]: a node storing only block headers that uses Merkle proofs to verify specific transactions, suitable for wallets and mobile devices.

**Liveness detection** [2]: techniques used during e-KYC to confirm a live person is present — such as blinking or head movements — defeating photos, videos and masks.

**Machine learning (ML)** [7]: a subset of AI in which computers learn patterns from data and improve at a task without being explicitly programmed with fixed rules.

**Malware** [8]: malicious software, such as fake apps or trojans, installed on a device to steal data or give attackers control.

**Market making** [5]: continuously quoting both bid and ask prices for a security to earn the bid–ask spread, while managing the risk of holding inventory.

**Maysir** [9]: gambling or purely speculative gain where one party profits from another's loss by chance; prohibited under Shariah.

**Mean reversion** [5]: a trading approach based on the tendency of prices that move abnormally far from their historical average to return toward that average.

**Merkle tree** [4]: a tree of pairwise hashes over a block's transactions, ending in a single root that enables efficient, lightweight verification of individual transactions.

**Mining** [4]: the competitive process of validating transactions and adding blocks to a proof-of-work blockchain in return for block rewards and fees.

**Mobile-first** [2]: a design philosophy in which the smartphone application is the primary banking channel and all other channels are built around it.

**Model risk** [7]: the risk of loss or customer harm from models that are wrong, misused or applied outside their valid range.

**Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT)** [5]: Markowitz's framework showing that combining assets with less-than-perfect correlation can achieve the best expected return for a given level of risk.

**Momentum strategy** [5]: a trading approach that buys assets whose prices have risen and sells those that have fallen, assuming trends persist; often implemented with moving-average crossovers.

**Mudarabah** [9]: a profit-sharing partnership where one party supplies capital and the other supplies management; losses fall on the capital provider.

**Mule account** [8]: a bank account used to receive and move criminal proceeds, often rented or sold by its legitimate holder to a syndicate.

**Murabahah** [9]: a cost-plus sale contract in which the financier buys an asset and resells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, payable in instalments.

**Musharakah** [9]: a joint partnership in which all parties contribute capital and share profits and losses by agreed ratios.

**Neobank** [1]: Global industry term for a digital-only, branchless banking provider; in Malaysia the nearest equivalents are the five fully licensed digital banks.

**Neural network** [7]: a computational structure of interconnected processing layers, loosely inspired by the brain, used in deep learning.

**Node** [4]: a computer running a blockchain's software that stores, relays and (depending on type) validates ledger data according to the consensus rules.

**Non-fungible token (NFT)** [4]: a unique blockchain token representing a specific item, not interchangeable one-for-one with other tokens.

**Nonce** [4]: an adjustable number in a block header that proof-of-work miners vary when searching for a hash that meets the network's difficulty target.

**Online banking** [1]: The provision of banking services over the internet as an additional channel of a traditional branch-based bank, such as Maybank2u or CIMB Clicks.

**Open banking** [2]: the consent-based sharing of customer banking data with authorised third-party providers through secure APIs.

**Open finance** [2]: the extension of consented, API-based data sharing beyond bank accounts to wider financial data such as investments and insurance.

**Operational resilience** [8]: an institution's ability to continue delivering critical operations and recover quickly during disruption.

**Operational risk** [8]: the risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events.

**Oracle problem** [4]: the limitation that blockchains cannot verify off-chain, real-world facts, so smart contracts must rely on trusted external data feeds called oracles.

**Origination fee** [3]: a charge levied by a platform for processing, assessing and arranging a financing facility, usually a percentage of the amount raised, deducted from the proceeds.

**Overfitting** [5]: tuning a model so precisely to historical data that it memorises noise rather than learning a durable pattern, causing live performance to collapse.

**Peer-to-peer (P2P) financing** [1]: The raising of debt funding by businesses from many investors through an online platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia.

**Permissioned blockchain** [4]: a network in which read and/or validation rights are restricted to approved participants, covering private and consortium designs.

**Personal data breach notification** [8]: the mandatory duty, introduced by the 2024 PDPA amendments, to notify authorities and affected individuals of serious data breaches.

**Phishing** [8]: fraudulent messages that impersonate trusted entities to trick recipients into revealing login credentials or personal data.

**Post-money valuation** [3]: a company's value immediately after a funding round, equal to the pre-money valuation plus the amount of new capital raised.

**Post-quantum cryptography** [6]: Cryptographic methods designed to resist attacks from both conventional and sufficiently capable quantum computers.

**Pre-money valuation** [3]: the agreed value of a company immediately before new investment money is added in a funding round.

**Private key** [4]: a secret cryptographic number that proves ownership of cryptocurrency and authorises spending; losing it means losing the funds permanently.

**Proof of stake** [4]: a consensus mechanism where validators lock up cryptocurrency as collateral to propose and confirm blocks, with dishonesty punished by slashing.

**Proof of stake (PoS)** [4]: a consensus mechanism in which validators lock up cryptocurrency as a stake and are penalised for dishonesty, avoiding PoW's energy race.

**Proof of work** [4]: a consensus mechanism requiring participants to expend computational effort to add blocks, making attacks economically costly.

**Proof of work (PoW)** [4]: a consensus mechanism in which miners compete to solve a computational puzzle, making block production costly and attacks economically impractical.

**Public key** [4]: a cryptographic value derived from a private key, used to generate receiving addresses; safe to share.

**Public-key cryptography** [4]: an encryption scheme using a linked private key (secret, used to sign) and public key (shared, used to verify), forming the basis of blockchain ownership.

**Quantum computing** [6]: A computing approach using quantum-mechanical properties, with potential advantages for selected computational problems.

**Real-time gross settlement (RTGS)** [2]: the settlement of high-value interbank payments individually, immediately and with finality, rather than in netted batches.

**Rebalancing** [5]: restoring a portfolio to its target asset allocation by selling overweight assets and buying underweight ones, on a calendar or threshold basis.

**Recognized Market Operator (RMO)** [3]: an electronic platform operator registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia to run a market — such as P2P, ECF or digital asset trading — under the Guidelines on Recognized Markets.

**RegTech** [8]: the use of technology by regulated firms to meet regulatory and compliance obligations more efficiently and effectively.

**Regulatory sandbox** [1]: A supervised framework allowing firms to test innovative financial services with real customers under relaxed conditions and safeguards before full licensing.

**Reinforcement learning** [7]: ML in which an agent learns by trial and error, receiving rewards or penalties to discover optimal strategies.

**Remittance** [2]: a cross-border transfer of funds, typically sent by migrant workers to family members in their home country.

**Riba** [9]: interest or usury; any guaranteed return on money lent, prohibited in Islamic finance because returns must be earned through real trade or investment.

**Risk profiling** [5]: the process of assessing an investor's ability and willingness to bear investment risk so that a suitable portfolio can be recommended.

**Robo-advisory** [5]: automated, algorithm-driven investment advice and portfolio management delivered through a digital platform with minimal human supervision.

**Security token offering (STO)** [4]: a regulated issuance of blockchain tokens representing legally recognised securities, subject to securities law and disclosure requirements.

**Sensor data** [6]: Measurements collected by a device, such as location, movement or usage, which may be incomplete or inaccurate.

**Shariah Advisory Council (SAC)** [9]: the apex authority under the Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009 for ascertaining Islamic law for Islamic financial business in Malaysia.

**Shariah-compliant** [3]: structured in accordance with Islamic law, avoiding prohibited elements such as riba (interest) through contracts like murabahah, tawarruq or wakalah.

**Signal** [5]: the output of a trading algorithm's rules — an instruction to buy, sell or hold — generated from market data such as prices and volumes.

**Slippage** [5]: the difference between a trade's expected execution price and its actual execution price, arising from market movement and limited liquidity.

**Smart contract** [4]: a self-executing program stored on a blockchain that automatically enforces the terms of an agreement when coded conditions are met.

**Social engineering** [8]: manipulating people, rather than attacking systems, into divulging information or performing actions that compromise security.

**Stablecoin** [2]: a crypto-asset designed to maintain stable value against a reference asset, usually a fiat currency; fiat-collateralised examples include USDT and USDC.

**Sukuk** [9]: Shariah-compliant investment certificates representing ownership of underlying assets or ventures, generating returns from those assets rather than interest.

**Supervised learning** [7]: ML trained on labelled historical examples to predict outcomes for new cases, such as default or fraud.

**SupTech** [8]: the use of technology by regulators and supervisors themselves to monitor and supervise the financial industry more effectively.

**Takaful** [9]: Islamic insurance based on mutual contributions to a common fund that compensates members who suffer loss, avoiding gharar and maysir.

**Tawarruq** [9]: monetisation via commodity murabahah; buying a commodity on deferred terms and selling it for cash to obtain liquidity.

**Three lines of defence** [8]: a governance model assigning risk ownership to business units, oversight to risk and compliance, and independent assurance to internal audit.

**Tokenisation** [4]: the creation of a blockchain-based digital token representing ownership of, or a claim on, a financial or real-world underlying asset.

**Tokenised deposit** [2]: an ordinary commercial-bank deposit represented as a programmable digital token transferable on modern ledgers.

**Transaction monitoring** [7]: the screening of customer transactions for patterns consistent with money laundering or terrorism financing, as required under AMLA 2001.

**Unspent transaction output (UTXO)** [4]: a discrete, spendable chunk of cryptocurrency recorded on the blockchain, consumed entirely as an input when spent, with any remainder returned as change.

**Unsupervised learning** [7]: ML that finds structure, clusters or anomalies in unlabelled data without known correct answers.

**Usage-based insurance** [6]: Insurance pricing or coverage informed by measured use or behaviour, such as driving distance or patterns.

**Value-based Intermediation (VBI)** [9]: BNM's 2018 initiative with the Islamic banking industry to deliver measurable value to stakeholders, the economy and the community.

**Virtual card** [2]: a payment card credential that exists only in digital form, issued instantly inside an app and usable for online or wallet-based payments.

**Virtual reality (VR)** [6]: A computer-generated environment that immerses a user, commonly through a headset.

**Wakalah** [9]: an agency contract in which a principal appoints an agent to act on their behalf, typically for a fee; used in robo-advisory and takaful.

**Wearable** [6]: A body-worn connected device that may support payments or collect user or activity data.
