## Opening Vignette

At 7.30 a.m. in Kota Bharu, Kelantan, twenty-year-old diploma student Aina Sofia unlocks her phone before she unlocks her hostel room door. She pays for her nasi lemak breakfast by scanning the stallholder's DuitNow QR code with her Touch 'n Go eWallet, then sends RM50 to her mother in Bachok through her GXBank savings account. The money arrives in seconds, and neither of them has visited a bank branch in months. On the bus to campus, Aina checks a robo-advisory app, where the RM200 she saved last semester has been invested automatically according to her risk profile. Her uncle, who runs a small batik workshop, recently raised RM40,000 through a peer-to-peer financing platform after being turned away by two banks.

None of these services existed in Malaysia when Aina was born. This chapter explains what they are, where they came from, who provides and regulates them in Malaysia, how they make money, and whether they truly make finance fairer for everyone.

## 1.1 Scope of FinTech and Its Relationship to Digital Banking

Few pairs of words are used as loosely in business today as "digital banking" and "FinTech". News articles, bank advertisements and even regulators sometimes use them differently. Before we can study the Malaysian landscape, we need precise, citable definitions. This section defines financial technology, separates its two common meanings, defines digital banking, and distinguishes digital banking from online banking and neobanks.

### 1.1.1 Defining Financial Technology (FinTech)

**Financial technology (FinTech)** is defined as digital innovations and technology-enabled business model innovations in the financial sector (Puschmann, 2017). Two parts of this definition deserve attention. First, FinTech involves *digital innovation* — the application of computing, mobile communications and data analytics to financial problems. Second, and equally important, FinTech involves *business model innovation* — new ways of producing, distributing and charging for financial services, not merely faster versions of old processes. A bank that scans paper forms into a computer is digitising; a platform that replaces the bank's lending process entirely is a FinTech business model innovation.

Gomber, Kauffman, Parker and Weber (2018) interpret the phenomenon as three forces acting on financial services: *innovation* (new products and processes), *disruption* (new entrants displacing incumbents) and *transformation* (deep structural change in how finance operates). Thakor (2020) adds that FinTech matters economically because it can reduce the cost of producing financial services, improve the matching of providers and customers, and allow better use of data in decisions such as credit assessment. In short, FinTech is not a single product or app; it is an ongoing technological re-organisation of the financial sector.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** Do not confuse "FinTech" (the field or industry) with "a fintech" (a single company). In this book, *FinTech* refers to the field, while a firm operating in it is called a *fintech company* or *fintech start-up*.

### 1.1.2 FinTech-as-Industry versus FinTech-as-Technology

The word "FinTech" is used in two distinct ways, and recognising both will help you read reports and job advertisements critically.

- **FinTech-as-industry.** In this sense, FinTech is a *sector of the economy* made up of firms — usually young, technology-led start-ups — that deliver financial services such as payments, lending, fund-raising, investment or insurance. When a report says "Malaysia's fintech industry is growing", it means this population of firms. Examples in Malaysia include Funding Societies in peer-to-peer financing, MoneyMatch in cross-border remittance, and HelloGold in Shariah-compliant digital gold savings.
- **FinTech-as-technology.** In this sense, FinTech is a *toolbox of technologies* applied to finance: mobile applications, cloud computing, big data analytics, artificial intelligence (AI), application programming interfaces (APIs), biometrics and distributed ledger technology (DLT). When an incumbent bank announces it is "investing in fintech", it usually means adopting these technologies, not buying start-ups.

The two meanings interact. Start-ups in the FinTech *industry* are typically built entirely on FinTech *technologies*, while incumbent banks increasingly deploy the same technologies to defend their markets (Puschmann, 2017; Lee & Shin, 2018). Throughout this book, context will indicate which meaning is intended, but as a student you should always ask: is the speaker talking about the firms, or the technology?

### 1.1.3 Defining Digital Banking

Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM), the central bank, provides the authoritative local definition. Under its licensing framework, a **digital bank** is a bank licensed under the Financial Services Act 2013 or the Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 that conducts banking business *wholly or almost wholly through digital or electronic means* (Bank Negara Malaysia [BNM], 2020). Building on this, **digital banking** can be defined as the delivery of regulated banking services — accepting deposits, providing financing or lending, and facilitating payments — through digital channels end-to-end, with customer onboarding, verification and servicing conducted remotely rather than at physical branches (BNM, 2020).

Three implications follow. First, digital banking in Malaysia is a *licensed, regulated* activity: a digital bank is still a bank, subject to capital, liquidity and conduct requirements. Second, the digital channel is the primary — often the only — channel; branch networks are absent or minimal. Third, remote identity verification is essential, which is why BNM's electronic know-your-customer (e-KYC) policy, first issued in 2020 and revised in April 2024, is a critical enabler of the model (BNM, 2024a).

### 1.1.4 Digital Banking versus Online Banking versus Neobanks

Students frequently use these three terms interchangeably, but they describe different things.

**Online banking** is the provision of banking services over the internet as an *additional channel* of a traditional, branch-based bank. The bank existed first; the website or app came later. Malaysia's first internet banking platform, Maybank2u, launched in 2000, and services such as CIMB Clicks remain examples of incumbent banks offering digital channels alongside their branches.

**Digital banking**, as defined in Section 1.1.3, describes a banking operation designed from the ground up to be digital: account opening via e-KYC, no reliance on branches, and products built for a smartphone-first customer (BNM, 2020). A licensed digital bank such as GXBank or Boost Bank is a regulated bank in its own right, not a channel of another bank.

A **neobank** is a term from global industry usage for a digital-only bank that operates without physical branches and typically targets customers through a mobile app. Internationally, some neobanks hold full banking licences (for example, UK challenger banks), while others operate by partnering with licensed banks. In Malaysia, however, the five digital banks all hold full BNM licences, so the closest local equivalent to the global "neobank" concept is the licensed digital bank itself (BNM, 2020).

Table 1.1 summarises the distinctions.

**Table 1.1: Online banking, digital banking and neobanks compared**

| Feature | Online banking | Licensed digital bank | Neobank (global usage) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A digital channel of an incumbent bank | A fully licensed bank operating almost wholly digitally | A digital-only banking provider |
| Licence | Held by the parent bank | Digital bank licence under FSA 2013 / IFSA 2013 | Varies: own licence or partner's licence |
| Physical branches | Yes, often extensive | None (or minimal) | None |
| Customer onboarding | May still require a branch visit for some products | Fully remote via e-KYC | Fully remote |

| Global examples | Internet portals of large banks | — | Chime (US), Revolut (UK/EU) |

## 1.2 Evolution of Financial Technology: From Early Digitisation to Platform-Based Finance

FinTech did not begin with the smartphone. In an influential framework, Arner, Barberis and Buckley (2016) divide the technological evolution of finance into three historical eras — FinTech 1.0, 2.0 and 3.0 (with a 3.5 variant for parts of Asia) — and subsequent literature, notably Gupta and Tham (2019), has extended the story with an emerging FinTech 4.0. Understanding this evolution matters because each era left behind institutions, regulations and habits that still shape Malaysia's financial system today.

### 1.2.1 FinTech 1.0: The Analogue Era (1866–1967)

Arner et al. (2016) date FinTech 1.0 from 1866, when the first successful transatlantic telegraph cable connected North America and Europe. For the first time, financial information — prices, orders, settlement instructions — could cross an ocean in minutes rather than weeks. Finance became *globalised* through telecommunications even though the underlying processes remained analogue and paper-based.

Other milestones of the era include the establishment of Fedwire in 1918, through which the United States Federal Reserve transferred funds electronically using telegraph lines and Morse code, and the launch of the Diners Club card in 1950, often described as the first widely used payment card. Arner et al. (2016) mark the end of FinTech 1.0 in 1967, when Barclays Bank installed the world's first automated teller machine (ATM) in London — a fitting symbol of the shift from analogue to digital technology in finance.

> **💡 DID YOU KNOW? (TRIVIA):** The first "electronic" money transfers were sent over telegraph wires more than a century ago. Fedwire, launched in 1918, moved money using Morse code — proof that the idea of fintech is far older than the internet (Arner et al., 2016).

### 1.2.2 FinTech 2.0: Digitalisation of Traditional Finance (1967–2008)

FinTech 2.0, from 1967 to 2008, is the era in which *traditional financial institutions* digitalised their own processes and delivery channels (Arner et al., 2016). The technology was new, but the providers were familiar: banks, insurers and exchanges remained firmly in control.

Global milestones included the spread of ATMs from 1967 onwards; the creation of SWIFT (the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) in 1973, which standardised secure interbank messaging across borders; the rise of electronic securities trading, exemplified by the NASDAQ electronic exchange from 1971; the introduction of mainframe computing into back offices; and, from the mid-1990s, the arrival of internet banking, with Security First Network Bank in the United States (1995) widely cited as the first internet-only bank.

Malaysia participated in this era. The central bank launched RENTAS (Real Time Electronic Transfer of Funds and Securities), the country's real-time gross settlement system, in 1999, allowing large interbank payments to be settled instantly and irrevocably. One year later, Maybank2u (2000) became Malaysia's first internet banking platform, bringing the FinTech 2.0 model — an incumbent bank adding a digital channel — to Malaysian retail customers.

### 1.2.3 FinTech 3.0 and 3.5: New Entrants Post-2008 and Asia's Rise

Arner et al. (2016) identify 2008 as a turning point for two converging reasons. First, the global financial crisis damaged public trust in incumbent banks and triggered heavier regulation of them, opening space for new entrants. Second, the smartphone — popularised by the iPhone's launch in 2007 — put a powerful, always-connected computer in consumers' pockets. FinTech 3.0 is therefore the era of *new entrants*: start-ups and technology firms delivering financial services directly to consumers, often bypassing banks altogether.

The hallmarks of the era appeared quickly: online payment services such as PayPal (founded 1998) and Alipay (launched 2004 in China); mobile money such as M-Pesa (2007, Kenya), which brought basic financial services to millions without bank accounts; peer-to-peer lending, pioneered by Zopa in the United Kingdom (2005); and, in 2008 itself, the publication of the Bitcoin whitepaper proposing a peer-to-peer electronic cash system outside the banking system entirely (Nakamoto, 2008).

Arner et al. (2016) add a special label, **FinTech 3.5**, for economies — above all China and India — that skipped the card-and-branch stages of development and leapfrogged directly to mobile-first financial services. In China, Alipay and WeChat Pay evolved into "super-apps" combining payments, lending, investment and insurance inside a single platform. Southeast Asia followed a similar path: in Malaysia, bank-led and non-bank e-wallets proliferated, the DuitNow instant payment service launched in 2018, and the DuitNow QR national QR standard followed in 2019, allowing any participating wallet or bank app to pay any participating merchant.

### 1.2.4 FinTech 4.0: Platformisation, BigTech, AI and Embedded Finance

Where does the story go next? Building on the FinTech 1.0–3.0 framework, subsequent literature — notably Gupta and Tham (2019) — frames an emerging **FinTech 4.0** era characterised by four mutually reinforcing forces.

1. **Platformisation.** Financial services are increasingly distributed through large digital *platforms* that host many providers and services in one ecosystem, rather than through stand-alone products sold by individual institutions. The platform — not the branch or even the app of a single bank — becomes the main point of contact with the customer.
2. **BigTech.** Large technology companies with vast user bases and data advantage — regionally, firms such as Grab and Sea — have moved into payments, lending and banking. Zetzsche, Buckley, Arner and Barberis (2017) describe this data-driven entry of technology firms into finance as "TechFin", the mirror image of FinTech.
3. **Artificial intelligence.** AI now supports credit scoring, fraud detection, customer service and personalised financial advice. In Malaysia, BNM issued a discussion paper on artificial intelligence in the financial sector in August 2025, signalling how central AI has become to regulatory thinking (BNM, 2025a).
4. **Embedded finance and tokenisation.** **Embedded finance** is the integration of financial services — payments, lending, insurance — directly into non-financial apps and platforms, so that, for example, a consumer can obtain financing at the checkout of an e-commerce app without ever visiting a bank. Alongside it, BNM's October 2025 discussion paper on asset tokenisation explores representing financial and real-world assets on programmable ledgers (BNM, 2025b).

A note of caution: eras overlap and labels are debated. FinTech 4.0 is an *emerging* framing from subsequent literature, not a settled historical fact, and elements of FinTech 2.0 and 3.0 remain firmly in place (Gupta & Tham, 2019). Table 1.2 summarises the evolution.

**Table 1.2: The evolution of FinTech eras**

| Era | Period | Character | Defining features | Malaysian milestones |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FinTech 1.0 | 1866–1967 | Analogue, globalising | Transatlantic cable (1866), Fedwire (1918), Diners Club (1950) | — |
| FinTech 2.0 | 1967–2008 | Incumbents digitalise | ATMs, SWIFT (1973), electronic trading, internet banking | RENTAS (1999); Maybank2u (2000) |
| FinTech 3.0 | 2008–present | New entrants | Smartphones, start-ups, mobile money, P2P, crypto | E-wallet growth; DuitNow (2018); DuitNow QR (2019) |
| FinTech 3.5 | 2010s | Asia leapfrogs | Super-apps, mobile-first models in China, India, SEA | QR-based merchant payments |
| FinTech 4.0 | Emerging | Platforms and intelligence | Platformisation, BigTech, AI, embedded finance, tokenisation | Five digital banks; BNM AI and tokenisation discussion papers (2025) |

*Sources: Arner et al. (2016); Gupta and Tham (2019); BNM (2025a, 2025b).*

## 1.3 The Malaysian FinTech Ecosystem and the Roles of Banks, FinTech Firms, Regulators and Payment Operators

A FinTech *ecosystem* is the network of regulators, market operators, industry bodies, infrastructure providers and firms whose interactions produce digital financial services (Lee & Shin, 2018). Malaysia's ecosystem is distinctive in two ways: it is shared between two powerful regulators, and it contains an unusually strong Islamic finance dimension. This section maps the key institutions and market segments.

### 1.3.1 Regulators and Ecosystem Enablers

**Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM)** is the central bank, established under the Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009, and regulates banks, payment systems, electronic money (e-money) and money services under the Financial Services Act 2013 (FSA 2013) and the Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 (IFSA 2013). Its major fintech-relevant instruments include:

- the **Licensing Framework for Digital Banks** (December 2020), which created the digital bank licence (BNM, 2020);
- the **Financial Sector Blueprint 2022–2026**, which sets five desired outcomes for the financial sector and targets roughly 15% compound annual growth in e-payments (BNM, 2022);
- the **Financial Technology Regulatory Sandbox Framework** (revised February 2024), which allows firms to test innovative services with real customers under relaxed but supervised conditions — a **regulatory sandbox** (BNM, 2024b);
- the **e-KYC policy** (2020, revised April 2024), enabling non-face-to-face customer onboarding (BNM, 2024a); and
- the **Risk Management in Technology (RMiT)** policy (2020; revised 2023; updated November 2025), which imposes technology and cyber-risk requirements on financial institutions (BNM, 2025c).

**Securities Commission Malaysia (SC)** regulates capital market fintech under the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007: peer-to-peer financing, equity crowdfunding, digital asset exchanges, digital investment management and initial exchange offering (IEO) operators, principally through the Guidelines on Recognized Markets and the Guidelines on Digital Assets (Securities Commission Malaysia [SC], 2024a, 2024b). Since the Capital Markets and Services (Prescription of Securities) (Digital Currency and Digital Token) Order 2019 took effect on 15 January 2019, digital currencies and digital tokens have been prescribed as securities in Malaysia — meaning they fall under the SC's jurisdiction and are *not* legal tender.

Around the two regulators sit important ecosystem enablers:

- **Payments Network Malaysia (PayNet)**, owned by BNM, operates the national shared payments infrastructure described in Section 1.3.3.
- **Malaysia Digital Economy Corporation (MDEC)** champions the growth of the digital economy, including fintech.
- The **Fintech Association of Malaysia** is the industry's main representative body.
- The **Malaysia International Islamic Financial Centre (MIFC)** promotes Malaysia as a global hub for Islamic finance, including Islamic fintech.

**Table 1.3: Key institutions in Malaysia's digital financial services ecosystem**

| Institution | Type | Role in the ecosystem |
|---|---|---|
| Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) | Regulator | Banks, payment systems, e-money, money services; digital bank licensing; sandbox; e-KYC; RMiT |
| Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) | Regulator | P2P financing, equity crowdfunding, digital asset exchanges, digital investment management, IEOs |
| PayNet | Infrastructure | Operates national payment rails (RENTAS, IBG, FPX, JomPAY, MyDebit, DuitNow, DuitNow QR) |
| MDEC | Government agency | Digital economy development and fintech promotion |
| Fintech Association of Malaysia | Industry body | Represents and connects fintech firms |
| MIFC | Promotion agency | Develops Malaysia as an international Islamic finance centre |

### 1.3.2 Key Segments of Malaysian FinTech

**Payments** is the largest and most visible segment. Malaysia recorded 18.4 billion e-payment transactions in 2025, up 25% from 14.7 billion in 2024 — an average of 538 transactions per Malaysian — with retail e-payment value reaching RM831 billion (BNM, 2026). **Electronic money (e-money)** is a digitally stored monetary value issued under BNM licence that can be used to pay for goods and services; the average e-money transaction was RM43 in 2025, up from RM33 in 2024 (BNM, 2026).

**E-wallets** — smartphone apps holding e-money — are the everyday face of this segment. Major Malaysian e-wallets include Touch 'n Go eWallet, MAE by Maybank, GrabPay, Boost, ShopeePay and BigPay.

**Digital banks.** Malaysia licensed five digital banks in 2022; their journey is told in this chapter's case study.

**Peer-to-peer (P2P) financing** is the raising of debt funding by businesses from many investors through a SC-registered online platform. Since 2016, Malaysian P2P platforms have raised a cumulative RM11.32 billion across 154,283 campaigns; 98% of campaigns fund working capital, 62% raise RM50,000 or less, and 26% of funds were Shariah-compliant in 2025 (SC, 2026). Example operators include Funding Societies, Fundaztic, B2B FinPAL, microLEAP, Alixco, CapBay and MoneySave.

**Equity crowdfunding (ECF)** is the raising of equity capital by companies from many investors through a SC-registered online platform. Cumulatively, RM917.51 million has been raised across 445 campaigns since 2016, mostly at seed and Series A stages; example platforms include pitchIN, Ata Plus, MyStartr, Ethis and Leet Capital (SC, 2026).

> **💡 DID YOU KNOW? (TRIVIA):** Malaysia was the first ASEAN country to regulate equity crowdfunding (2015) and among the first in the region to regulate P2P financing (2016). Its digital investment management framework of May 2017 was also the first robo-advisory licensing framework in the region (SC, 2017, 2024b).

**Digital investment management (DIM)** — commonly called robo-advisory — provides automated risk profiling, suitability assessment, asset allocation and portfolio rebalancing with minimal human supervision, licensed by the SC since May 2017 (SC, 2017). Nine DIM firms are registered, including StashAway (first licensed, 2018), Wahed Invest, MYTHEO, Raiz, Akru, Versa, Kenanga Digital Investing and BEST Invest. Although DIM assets remain below 1% of the RM1.07 trillion fund management industry (2024), the segment is growing — 15% growth in 2023 — with over one million accounts, 51% of them held by investors in their twenties (SC, 2026).

**Digital asset exchanges (DAX)** are SC-registered platforms for trading digital assets; registered operators include Luno Malaysia, SINEGY Technologies, Tokenize Technology and Hata Digital (SC, 2024a). Because digital assets are prescribed as securities under the 2019 Order, trading on registered DAXs is regulated — but crypto remains *not* legal tender in Malaysia.

**Insurtech** — the application of FinTech technologies to insurance and takaful — is a smaller but growing segment, with digital distribution and embedded insurance appearing inside e-wallets, e-commerce platforms and digital bank apps.

**Islamic fintech** deserves special emphasis. Malaysia is consistently ranked among the world's top Islamic fintech ecosystems, alongside Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Indonesia and Bahrain. The global Islamic fintech market reached approximately US$198 billion in transaction volume in 2024/25 and is projected to reach US$341 billion by 2029, with 484 Islamic fintech firms operating globally (DinarStandard & Elipses, 2026). Malaysian examples include HelloGold (Shariah-compliant digital gold savings), Ethis (Islamic P2P/ECF), Global Sadaqah (waqf and sadaqah platform), microLEAP, Wahed Invest, BEST Invest, and the Islamic digital banks AEON Bank and KAF Digital Bank. BNM's Value-based Intermediation initiative (2018) and Shariah standards underpin this segment, anchored by the MIFC.

> **🇲🇾 MALAYSIAN SPOTLIGHT:** DuitNow QR, Malaysia's national QR standard operated by PayNet, doubled its volume to roughly 3 billion transactions in 2025 and reached about 3 million merchant touchpoints — many of them micro, small and medium enterprises that previously accepted only cash. A single interoperable QR code now lets a customer pay a night-market stall from almost any bank app or e-wallet in the country (PayNet, 2026).

### 1.3.3 Payments Infrastructure: PayNet and the National Rails

Behind every tap, scan and transfer lies shared infrastructure operated mainly by PayNet. The principal national rails are:

| Rail | Launched | Function |
|---|---|---|
| RENTAS | 1999 | Real-time gross settlement of large-value interbank payments |
| Interbank GIRO (IBG) | — | Batch-based interbank fund transfers |
| FPX | — | Online bank-to-bank payments for e-commerce |
| JomPAY | 2015 | National bill payment service |
| MyDebit | — | Domestic debit card scheme |
| DuitNow | 2018 | Instant account-to-account transfers using proxies such as mobile numbers |
| DuitNow QR | 2019 | National interoperable QR payment standard |

Usage has grown explosively. PayNet processed 8.44 billion digital payment transactions in 2025 (PayNet, 2026). DuitNow QR has also gone regional: cross-border QR payment linkages are live with Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and China, with Cambodia added in 2025 and India expected in 2026; cross-border QR transactions grew 2.5 times in 2025 (PayNet, 2026). A Malaysian traveller in Bangkok can therefore pay a Thai merchant with the same app used at home — a quiet but remarkable piece of financial diplomacy built on shared rails.

## 1.4 FinTech Business Models, Customer Needs and Sources of Revenue

Technology alone does not explain the transformation of finance; equally important is *who* provides the services and *how* they earn revenue. This section identifies five groups of players and examines the business models that sustain them, drawing on Lee and Shin's (2018) analysis of fintech ecosystems and business models.

### 1.4.1 Key Players in the Digital Financial Services Arena

Five groups of players compete and cooperate in Malaysia's digital financial services arena.

1. **Incumbent banks digitising.** Established banks have responded to fintech competition by rebuilding their own services around mobile apps — for example, MAE by Maybank and CIMB Octo/CIMB Clicks. Their advantages are large customer bases, trusted brands and deposits; their weaknesses are legacy systems and branch cost structures (Thakor, 2020).
2. **Digital challenger banks.** The five licensed digital banks — GXBank, Boost Bank, AEON Bank, KAF Digital Bank and Ryt Bank — are branchless, app-native institutions built on modern technology stacks, targeting underserved and unserved segments (BNM, 2020).
3. **BigTech.** Large technology platforms embed financial services into ecosystems customers already use daily. In Malaysia this is no longer theoretical: Grab (with the Kuok Group) backs GXBank, and Sea (with YTL Digital Capital) backs Ryt Bank. Zetzsche et al. (2017) warn that BigTech's data advantage — knowing users' locations, purchases and social graphs — can shift competitive power decisively.
4. **Fintech start-ups.** Focused firms attack single pain points: remittances (MoneyMatch, a graduate of BNM's regulatory sandbox), SME financing (Funding Societies), gold savings (HelloGold) and crowdfunding (pitchIN). Their advantages are speed and specialisation; their challenge is achieving scale and profitability (Lee & Shin, 2018).
5. **Infrastructure providers.** Beneath the visible players sit firms and utilities that everyone else depends on: PayNet's national payment rails, e-KYC and credit-data services, cloud computing and core-banking technology vendors, and cybersecurity providers.

**Table 1.4: Five groups of players and their strategic goals**

| Player group | Malaysian examples | Main strengths | Strategic goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incumbent banks digitising | Maybank (MAE), CIMB (Octo/Clicks) | Customers, trust, deposits | Defend relationships by matching fintech convenience |
| Digital challenger banks | GXBank, Boost Bank, AEON Bank, KAF Digital Bank, Ryt Bank | Modern technology, low cost base | Win underserved segments profitably |
| BigTech | Grab (GXBank), Sea (Ryt Bank) | Data, daily-use platforms | Embed finance into existing ecosystems |
| Fintech start-ups | MoneyMatch, Funding Societies, HelloGold, pitchIN | Focus, speed | Solve one pain point better than banks |
| Infrastructure providers | PayNet, e-KYC and cloud/core-banking vendors | Scale, neutrality | Provide shared rails and services |

### 1.4.2 Business Models: How FinTech Firms Make Money

A **business model** describes how a firm creates, delivers and captures value. Lee and Shin (2018) show that fintech business models differ fundamentally from the traditional bank model of earning the spread between deposit and lending rates. Six revenue models dominate the Malaysian landscape.

1. **Payments fees.** Charging merchants or users per transaction — for example, the merchant discount rate (MDR) a merchant pays to accept card or QR payments.
2. **Interchange.** The **interchange fee** is the portion of a card transaction fee paid by the merchant's bank to the cardholder's bank (the issuer). Card-issuing digital banks and wallet providers earn interchange on every card spend.
3. **Subscription.** Recurring monthly or annual fees for premium features, used by some robo-advisors, business tools and premium account tiers.
4. **Marketplace/platform fees.** Platforms that match fund-raisers with investors charge listing, success or servicing fees. P2P and ECF operators in Malaysia earn revenue this way (SC, 2026).
5. **Lending margins.** Lending at a higher rate than the cost of funds — the classic banking model, also used by digital banks and some P2P-funded lenders.
6. **Freemium.** A **freemium** model offers basic services free to build a large user base, then charges for premium features or cross-sells other products. Many e-wallets and digital banks use free accounts as their entry point.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** Keep MDR and interchange distinct. The *merchant discount rate* is the total fee a merchant pays to accept a cashless payment; *interchange* is only the slice of that fee passed to the customer's bank. The remainder goes to the merchant's bank and the scheme or switch operator.

**Table 1.5: Major fintech business models with Malaysian illustrations**

| Model | How revenue is earned | Malaysian illustration | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payments fees (MDR) | Fee per merchant transaction | QR and card acceptance services | Thin margins; price competition |
| Interchange | Issuer's share of card transaction fees | Debit cards issued by digital banks | Depends on card usage volumes |
| Subscription | Recurring user fees | Premium tiers of investment/finance apps | Users resist paying for finance apps |
| Marketplace/platform | Listing, success, servicing fees | P2P and ECF platforms (SC-registered RMOs) | Needs steady deal flow both sides |
| Lending margins | Spread between lending and funding costs | Digital bank financing products | Constrained for digital banks by the RM3 billion asset cap |
| Freemium | Free basic service; paid upgrades/cross-sell | E-wallets and digital banks' entry accounts | Monetisation is uncertain and slow |

In practice, successful firms combine several models. A digital bank, for instance, may offer free accounts (freemium) to acquire customers, earn interchange on card spend, collect lending margins on financing, and later add subscription or platform income. Diversification matters because no single fintech revenue line has proven sufficient on its own at small scale (Lee & Shin, 2018).

## 1.5 Benefits, Operating Challenges and Barriers to Financial Inclusion

Is the fintech transformation actually good for Malaysians? The honest answer is: mostly yes, with important qualifications. This section weighs the documented benefits — cost efficiency, convenience and financial inclusion — against the challenges of the digital divide, trust, regulation and, especially for new providers, operational viability.

### 1.5.1 Benefits: Cost Efficiency, Convenience and Inclusion

**Cost efficiency.** Digital delivery removes branches and paper from the cost base, so services can be produced at a fraction of the traditional cost (Thakor, 2020). These savings can be passed on as cheaper remittances, lower account fees and micro-sized products — such as robo-advisory accounts with minimal balances or P2P notes of a few hundred ringgit — that would be uneconomical to serve at a branch.

**Convenience and speed.** e-KYC allows an account to be opened from a phone in minutes (BNM, 2024a); DuitNow moves money between accounts in seconds; bill payment, investment and insurance can be done at any hour. Convenience is not a trivial benefit: for a hawker or small trader, a day spent travelling to a branch is a day of lost income.

**Financial inclusion.** **Financial inclusion** is defined as the access of individuals and businesses to useful and affordable financial products and services — transactions, payments, savings, credit and insurance — delivered in a responsible and sustainable way (World Bank, 2022). The World Bank's Global Findex database shows Malaysia's remarkable progress in adult (15+) account ownership:

**Table 1.6: Account ownership in Malaysia, adults aged 15+ (%)**

| 2011 | 2014 | 2017 | 2021 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 66.2% | 80.7% | 85.3% | 88.4% | 88.7% |

*Source: World Bank Global Findex Database (World Bank, 2022, 2025).*

By 2024 the gender gap in Malaysian account ownership had narrowed to less than one percentage point, and even among the poorest 40% of households, ownership reached 82.0% (World Bank, 2025). Digital services reinforce these gains: DuitNow QR has brought cashless acceptance to around 3 million merchant touchpoints, many of them formerly cash-only micro, small and medium enterprises (PayNet, 2026), while P2P financing has channelled RM11.32 billion largely to working-capital needs of smaller businesses — 99% of them non-tech firms — that banks often overlook (SC, 2026). Thakor (2020) notes that alternative data can extend credit to "thin-file" borrowers with no conventional credit history, which is precisely the segment Malaysia's digital banks were licensed to serve (BNM, 2020).

### 1.5.2 Challenges: Digital Divide, Trust, Regulation and Profitability

**The digital divide.** Inclusion statistics can mask exclusion: elderly Malaysians, rural communities with weak connectivity, and those with low digital literacy may find app-only finance harder, not easier, to use. When branches close in favour of apps, the customers least able to adapt can be left behind — a risk that regulators expect providers to manage (BNM, 2022).

**Trust and fraud.** Digital finance moves money instantly, which criminals exploit. Malaysia has responded with the National Scam Response Centre (hotline 997, established 2022) to rapidly freeze scam-linked funds, and BNM has directed banks to tighten controls such as kill-switches to freeze accounts, blocking SMS one-time passwords for high-risk transactions, and binding accounts to one secured device. Trust remains fragile: a single widely publicised scam can push cautious consumers back to cash.

**Regulatory burden.** Compliance is expensive. Financial institutions must meet RMiT technology and cyber-resilience requirements (BNM, 2025c), anti-money-laundering obligations, and personal data protection duties under the Personal Data Protection Act 2010 (amended 2024). For start-ups, these fixed costs can be the difference between viability and failure — even though the same rules protect consumers.

**Operational viability of digital banks.** The sharpest challenge concerns the five digital banks themselves. During a **foundational phase** of three to five years, each digital bank's total assets are capped at RM3 billion (BNM, 2020). The cap protects financial stability while the banks prove themselves, but it also limits the size of their financing books — and therefore the lending-margin income that funds a traditional bank. With heavy upfront technology investment, free customer acquisition and constrained balance sheets, profitability is a genuine challenge. Global experience is sobering: many digital-only banks elsewhere have taken years to break even (Thakor, 2020). BNM's Governor noted in 2025 that the digital banks remain in their foundational phase, subject to asset size limitations, with deposits growing from underserved and unserved segments and credit products rolling out gradually (BNM, 2026).

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** An asset cap limits the *balance sheet*, not the number of customers. A digital bank can still grow deposits, accounts and fee income beyond the cap's shadow — which is why fee-based business models (Section 1.4.2) matter so much to its survival.

The central policy tension of Malaysian digital banking is now clear: the same licence conditions that push digital banks towards *financial inclusion* (serving small, remote, thin-file customers) make *operational viability* harder, because such customers are costly to serve and generate small balances. Whether Malaysia's digital banks can resolve this tension — through fee income, ecosystem partnerships and disciplined cost control — is one of the most important questions this book will follow.

## Case Study 1.1: Malaysia's Five Digital Banks — The Licensing Journey

In December 2020, in the middle of the COVID-19 pandemic, Bank Negara Malaysia issued its Licensing Framework for Digital Banks, inviting applications for up to five licences. The policy objective was explicit: digital banks should serve the *unserved and underserved* — micro-enterprises, gig workers, low-income and rural Malaysians — using technology rather than branches (BNM, 2020). To manage risk, licensees would operate under a foundational phase of three to five years with total assets capped at RM3 billion.

On 29 April 2022, BNM announced the five winners, each a consortium combining financial experience with technology and distribution muscle:

- **GXBank**, led by Grab together with the Kuok Group;
- **Boost Bank**, uniting Boost/Axiata with RHB Banking Group;
- **AEON Bank**, formed by AEON Financial Service, AEON Credit and MoneyLion;
- **KAF Digital Bank**, a consortium anchored by KAF Investment Bank with Carsome, MoneyMatch, Jirnexu and StoreHub; and
- **Ryt Bank**, pairing Sea Ltd with YTL Digital Capital.

The licensees then had to build their banks — systems, compliance, products — and satisfy BNM's readiness requirements before opening to the public. **GXBank** was the first to begin operations, on 1 September 2023. **Boost Bank** commenced on 15 January 2024. **AEON Bank** launched on 26 May 2024 as Malaysia's first Islamic digital bank, carrying the inclusion mandate into Shariah-compliant banking. Finally, **KAF Digital Bank** — the second Islamic digital bank — and **Ryt Bank** were both approved to commence operations on 20 December 2024 (BNM, 2026).

Early progress, while deliberately measured, fits the policy design. BNM reported that digital banks' deposits are growing from underserved and unserved segments, with credit products being rolled out gradually under the foundational phase's asset limitations (BNM, 2026). The journey illustrates three themes of this chapter: the shift from FinTech 3.0 start-ups to FinTech 4.0 platform-backed banking (BigTech names appear throughout the shareholder lists); the centrality of regulation in shaping market structure (only five licences, tightly conditioned); and the unresolved question of whether inclusion-oriented digital banking can also be commercially viable under a RM3 billion asset cap.

## Chapter Summary

- **1.1** FinTech is defined as digital innovations and technology-enabled business model innovations in the financial sector (Puschmann, 2017); the term is used both for an *industry* of firms and for a *toolbox of technologies*.
- **1.1** A digital bank conducts banking business wholly or almost wholly through digital or electronic means under an FSA 2013 or IFSA 2013 licence (BNM, 2020); digital banking differs from online banking (a channel of an incumbent) and from the global neobank concept.
- **1.2** Arner et al. (2016) trace FinTech 1.0 (1866–1967, analogue globalisation), FinTech 2.0 (1967–2008, incumbents digitalising) and FinTech 3.0/3.5 (post-2008 new entrants and Asia's mobile-first leapfrog).
- **1.2** Subsequent literature frames an emerging FinTech 4.0 era of platformisation, BigTech, AI and embedded finance (Gupta & Tham, 2019).
- **1.3** Malaysia's ecosystem is co-regulated by BNM (banks, payments, e-money) and the SC (P2P, ECF, DAX, DIM, IEOs), supported by PayNet, MDEC, the Fintech Association of Malaysia and MIFC.
- **1.3** Key segments — payments, digital banks, e-wallets, P2P/ECF, DIM, DAX, insurtech and Islamic fintech — show rapid, well-documented growth, including 18.4 billion e-payment transactions in 2025 (BNM, 2026).
- **1.4** Five player groups compete and cooperate: incumbent banks digitising, digital challenger banks, BigTech, fintech start-ups and infrastructure providers.
- **1.4** Fintech business models include payments fees, interchange, subscription, marketplace/platform fees, lending margins and freemium; sustainable firms usually combine several (Lee & Shin, 2018).
- **1.5** Benefits include cost efficiency, convenience and financial inclusion — Malaysian adult account ownership rose from 66.2% (2011) to 88.7% (2024) (World Bank, 2022, 2025).
- **1.5** Challenges include the digital divide, trust and fraud, regulatory compliance costs, and the operational viability of digital banks constrained by the RM3 billion asset cap during their foundational phase (BNM, 2020).

## Key Terms

- **BigTech**: Large technology companies with vast user bases and data advantages that expand into financial services such as payments, lending and banking.
- **Business model**: The plan describing how a firm creates, delivers and captures value, including its sources of revenue and cost structure.
- **Digital asset exchange (DAX)**: A platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia on which digital assets, prescribed as securities under Malaysian law, may be traded.
- **Digital banking**: The delivery of licensed banking services wholly or almost wholly through digital or electronic means, with remote onboarding and no reliance on branches.
- **Digital investment management (DIM)**: SC-licensed automated investment services — robo-advisory — covering risk profiling, suitability assessment, asset allocation and rebalancing with minimal human supervision.
- **Electronic know-your-customer (e-KYC)**: Regulator-approved digital processes for verifying a customer's identity remotely, enabling non-face-to-face account opening.
- **Electronic money (e-money)**: Digitally stored monetary value issued under a BNM licence that holders can use to pay merchants or transfer to other users.
- **Embedded finance**: The integration of financial services such as payments, lending or insurance directly into non-financial apps and platforms at the point of need.
- **Equity crowdfunding (ECF)**: The raising of equity capital by companies from many investors through an online platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia.
- **E-wallet**: A smartphone application that stores e-money and enables payments, transfers and often additional services such as investments or insurance.
- **Financial inclusion**: Access of individuals and businesses to useful, affordable financial products and services — payments, savings, credit and insurance — delivered responsibly and sustainably.
- **Financial technology (FinTech)**: Digital innovations and technology-enabled business model innovations in the financial sector; used for both the industry of firms and the underlying technologies.
- **Freemium**: A business model offering basic services free to build a large user base, then charging for premium features or cross-selling other products.
- **Interchange fee**: The portion of a card transaction fee paid by the merchant's bank to the cardholder's issuing bank, forming a key revenue source for card issuers.
- **Neobank**: Global industry term for a digital-only, branchless banking provider; in Malaysia the nearest equivalents are the five fully licensed digital banks.
- **Online banking**: The provision of banking services over the internet as an additional channel of a traditional branch-based bank, such as Maybank2u or CIMB Clicks.
- **Peer-to-peer (P2P) financing**: The raising of debt funding by businesses from many investors through an online platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia.
- **Regulatory sandbox**: A supervised framework allowing firms to test innovative financial services with real customers under relaxed conditions and safeguards before full licensing.

## Problem-Solving Corner

### Problem 1: Interchange Revenue of a Digital Bank

A licensed digital bank issues a debit card linked to its savings account. In one month, cardholders make 2,000,000 purchase transactions with an average value of RM50. Assume, purely for illustration, that the bank earns an average interchange fee of 0.5% of transaction value. The bank's monthly processing and servicing cost for the card programme is RM450,000.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly card spending volume, monthly interchange revenue, and annual interchange revenue.
**(b)** Calculate the net monthly contribution of the card programme.
**(c)** The bank's total operating cost is RM30 million per year. Comment on whether interchange alone can sustain the bank.

**Solution**

**(a)** Monthly spending volume = 2,000,000 × RM50 = **RM100,000,000**.
Monthly interchange revenue = RM100,000,000 × 0.5% = **RM500,000**.
Annual interchange revenue = RM500,000 × 12 = **RM6,000,000 (RM6 million)**.

**(b)** Net monthly contribution = RM500,000 − RM450,000 = **RM50,000** (RM600,000 per year).

**(c)** Annual net contribution from cards (RM0.6 million) covers only 2% of the RM30 million operating cost. Interchange alone cannot sustain the bank. The bank must add other revenue lines — lending margins (within the RM3 billion asset cap), subscriptions, marketplace or partnership fees — illustrating why successful fintech firms combine several business models (Lee & Shin, 2018).

### Problem 2: Measuring Financial Inclusion Progress

The Global Findex reports Malaysian adult account ownership of 66.2% in 2011 and 88.7% in 2024 (World Bank, 2022, 2025). For parts (b)–(c), *assume* an adult population of 24.0 million in both years (an illustrative simplification).

**(a)** Calculate the change in account ownership in percentage points and as a percentage increase relative to 2011.
**(b)** Estimate the number of adults *without* an account in 2011 and in 2024.
**(c)** By how much did the estimated unbanked adult population fall?

**Solution**

**(a)** Change in percentage points = 88.7% − 66.2% = **22.5 percentage points**.
Relative increase = 22.5 ÷ 66.2 × 100% ≈ **34.0%**.

**(b)** Unbanked share in 2011 = 100% − 66.2% = 33.8% → 33.8% × 24.0 million ≈ **8.11 million adults**.
Unbanked share in 2024 = 100% − 88.7% = 11.3% → 11.3% × 24.0 million ≈ **2.71 million adults**.

**(c)** Reduction ≈ 8.11 − 2.71 = **5.40 million fewer unbanked adults** (under the constant-population assumption; in reality population grew, so interpret this as an illustration of method).

### Problem 3: The RM3 Billion Asset Cap and Growth Planning

Digital bank *Bank Maya* (a fictional example) is in its foundational phase with total assets of RM2.7 billion, of which RM2.2 billion is its financing (loan) book. Its business plan calls for growing the financing book by 25% next year. BNM's asset limit is RM3 billion (BNM, 2020).

**(a)** If the plan is executed and all growth appears as new financing assets, what would total assets become? Does the bank breach the cap, and by how much?
**(b)** What is the maximum financing growth (in RM and %) the bank can book without breaching the cap, assuming other assets stay constant?
**(c)** Suggest two strategies that would let Bank Maya keep growing income without breaching the cap.

**Solution**

**(a)** New financing = 25% × RM2.2 billion = RM0.55 billion → total assets = RM2.7 + RM0.55 = **RM3.25 billion**. This **breaches the cap by RM250 million** (RM3.25b − RM3.0b).

**(b)** Available headroom = RM3.0b − RM2.7b = **RM300 million**. Maximum financing growth = RM300 million ÷ RM2.2 billion ≈ **13.6%**, not 25%.

**(c)** Strategies: (i) pivot towards *fee-based income* — payments, interchange, subscriptions, marketplace or referral fees — which adds revenue without adding assets; (ii) *distribute rather than hold* financing, e.g., originate financing and sell or refer it to partners, or focus on smaller, higher-yield Shariah-compliant or unsecured products within the headroom. Both keep growth inside the foundational-phase constraint while building a path to operational viability.
