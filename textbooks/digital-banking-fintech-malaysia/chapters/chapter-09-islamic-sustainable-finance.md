## 9.1 Islamic Digital Finance and Its Development

Islamic finance is one of the fastest-growing segments of the global financial system, and Malaysia is widely recognised as one of its leaders. When financial technology (FinTech) meets Islamic finance, the result is Islamic digital finance — a field in which Malaysia holds a top-two position among global ecosystems. This section explains the Shariah principles behind Islamic finance, defines Islamic digital finance, and traces the legal and regulatory foundations that Malaysia has built over several decades.

### 9.1.1 Core Shariah Principles in Finance

Islamic finance refers to financial activities that comply with **Shariah**, the body of Islamic law derived from the Quran and the Sunnah (the traditions of the Prophet Muhammad). Several core principles distinguish it from conventional finance (Oseni & Ali, 2019).

First is the prohibition of **riba**, usually translated as interest or usury. Money is regarded as a medium of exchange, not a commodity to be rented out for a guaranteed return. A conventional loan that charges 8% interest per annum regardless of the borrower's circumstances is therefore not permissible. Instead, returns must be earned through trade, investment or the sharing of risk.

Second is the prohibition of **gharar**, meaning excessive uncertainty or ambiguity in a contract. The object, price, delivery terms and obligations of each party must be clear. Selling something one does not own, or entering a contract whose outcome depends on unknown future events, introduces unacceptable gharar.

Third is the prohibition of **maysir**, which refers to gambling and speculative gain. Transactions that resemble betting — where one party's gain comes purely from another's loss by chance — are forbidden. This principle is why conventional insurance and highly speculative derivatives raise Shariah concerns.

Two positive principles complete the picture. **Risk-sharing** requires parties to a financial arrangement to share profits and losses rather than transferring all risk to one side, as in partnership contracts such as mudarabah and musharakah. **Asset-backing** requires that financial transactions be linked to real, identifiable assets or economic activities, so that finance serves the real economy rather than circulating money for its own sake (Oseni & Ali, 2019).

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** Islamic finance is not "interest-free conventional finance". A bank may earn a profit, but it must do so through a real transaction — buying and selling an asset, leasing it, or sharing business risk — not by simply charging interest on money lent.

### 9.1.2 Defining Islamic Digital Finance

Islamic digital finance can be defined as the application of financial technologies — such as mobile platforms, application programming interfaces (APIs), artificial intelligence, big data analytics and distributed ledger technology — to deliver financial products and services that comply with Shariah principles (Oseni & Ali, 2019). In simpler terms, it is FinTech that passes the Shariah test: the delivery channel is digital, and the underlying contract remains compliant.

The definition has two parts, and both matter. A mobile app that merely sells a conventional interest-based loan to Muslim customers is digital finance, but not Islamic finance. Conversely, a perfectly compliant murabahah (cost-plus sale) contract signed only on paper at a branch is Islamic finance, but not digital. Islamic digital finance exists only when both conditions — digital delivery and Shariah compliance — are satisfied together.

### 9.1.3 Malaysia's Leadership and Legal Foundations

Malaysia did not become an Islamic finance leader by accident. The country spent more than four decades building a complete ecosystem of institutions, laws and standards. A key institutional anchor is the **Malaysia International Islamic Financial Centre (MIFC)**, established in 2006 to promote Malaysia as an international marketplace for Islamic finance and to connect its industry with global markets.

Three pillars of the legal framework deserve special attention:

1. **Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 (IFSA).** This Act governs Islamic financial institutions in Malaysia and requires them to ensure, at all times, that their aims, operations, business, affairs and activities comply with Shariah. It gives statutory force to Shariah compliance rather than leaving it as a marketing claim (Islamic Financial Services Act 2013).
2. **Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009 (CBMA).** This Act makes the **Shariah Advisory Council (SAC)** of Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) the apex authority for the ascertainment of Islamic law for Islamic financial business. Where a court or arbitrator faces a Shariah question concerning Islamic finance, the SAC's rulings prevail (Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009). This centralisation gives the industry certainty and consistency.
3. **BNM Shariah standards.** BNM issues detailed policy documents on individual contracts — for example the standards on Murabahah, Tawarruq, Wakalah and Baiʿ al-Sarf — which set out the operational and Shariah requirements that Islamic financial institutions must observe when structuring products on those contracts (Bank Negara Malaysia [BNM], 2018a).

This combination — a statutory compliance duty, an apex Shariah authority and contract-level standards — is frequently cited as the reason global investors trust Malaysian Islamic finance, and it extends naturally into the digital sphere: an Islamic digital bank or a Shariah-compliant P2P platform in Malaysia operates under the same architecture.

> **🇲🇾 MALAYSIAN SPOTLIGHT:** The GIFT Report 2025/26 ranks the world's leading Islamic fintech ecosystems as Saudi Arabia and Malaysia at the top, followed by the United Arab Emirates, Indonesia and Bahrain. Malaysia's position rests on the MIFC platform, BNM's Shariah governance framework and a deep talent pool in Islamic finance (DinarStandard & Elipses, 2026).

## 9.2 FinTech Applications in Islamic Banking and Financial Services

The principles in Section 9.1 come alive in real products. Malaysia now has Islamic digital banks, Shariah-compliant P2P financing and equity crowdfunding (ECF) platforms, digital gold and waqf applications, and automated Shariah-screening tools. This section tours the main application areas.

### 9.2.1 Islamic Digital Banks

When BNM issued its *Licensing Framework for Digital Banks* in December 2020, it deliberately left room for Islamic models, requiring any digital bank offering Islamic products to comply fully with Shariah requirements (BNM, 2020). Of the five licences announced in April 2022, two went to Islamic digital banks:

- **AEON Bank** (a consortium of AEON Financial Service, AEON Credit Service and MoneyLion) launched on 26 May 2024 as **Malaysia's first Islamic digital bank**.
- **KAF Digital Bank** (led by KAF Investment Bank with partners including Carsome, MoneyMatch, Jirnexu and StoreHub) became the second Islamic digital bank, approved to commence operations on 20 December 2024 (BNM, 2026).

Like conventional digital banks, Islamic digital banks operate without physical branches and onboard customers remotely using electronic know-your-customer (e-KYC) processes under BNM's e-KYC policy (BNM, 2024). During a foundational phase of three to five years, they are subject to an asset limit of RM3 billion, which allows BNM to observe their risk management while they grow (BNM, 2020). The difference lies in the contracts: deposits and financing are structured on Shariah-compliant contracts such as wadiʿah (safe-keeping), qard (benevolent loan), tawarruq (commodity murabahah) or wakalah (agency), and each bank must maintain a Shariah committee and comply with IFSA's compliance duty.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** An Islamic digital bank is still a bank. Customer deposits placed in Shariah-compliant accounts with licensed banks are protected by Perbadanan Insurans Deposit Malaysia (PIDM) up to the applicable limit, just like conventional deposits — the "Islamic" label changes the contract, not the regulatory safety net.

### 9.2.2 Shariah-Compliant P2P Financing and Equity Crowdfunding

Peer-to-peer (P2P) financing platforms connect businesses seeking funds directly with investors through an online marketplace, regulated in Malaysia by the Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) under the *Guidelines on Recognized Markets* (Securities Commission Malaysia [SC], 2024b). Shariah-compliant P2P platforms do the same thing, but structure each financing note using Islamic contracts — commonly murabahah, tawarruq or wakalah — and maintain Shariah advisors to certify their processes.

The segment is meaningful in size. Of the RM11.32 billion raised cumulatively through Malaysian P2P platforms since 2016, **26% of funds in 2025 were Shariah-compliant** (Securities Commission Malaysia [SC], 2026). **microLEAP** is an example of a P2P operator that offers Shariah-compliant investment notes alongside conventional ones, allowing issuers and investors who require compliance to participate in the same market.

In equity crowdfunding — where investors receive shares rather than notes — the Shariah-compliant share is smaller but present: about **4.1% of ECF funds** raised in 2025 were Shariah-compliant (SC, 2026). **Ethis**, a Malaysia-linked Islamic crowdfunding platform, channels investment into vetted ventures and social impact projects using Islamic structures, demonstrating that crowd-based finance can carry explicit ethical and religious objectives.

> **💡 DID YOU KNOW? (TRIVIA):** Malaysia was the first country in ASEAN to regulate equity crowdfunding (2015) and among the first to regulate P2P financing (2016) — an early-mover advantage that gave Shariah-compliant variants a regulated home from the start (SC, 2026).

### 9.2.3 Digital Gold, Waqf, Sadaqah and Zakat Platforms

Islamic social finance — zakat (obligatory almsgiving), sadaqah (voluntary charity) and waqf (endowment) — has been transformed by digital channels.

- **HelloGold**, established in 2015, offers Shariah-compliant digital gold savings: users buy small amounts of fully allocated physical gold through an app, with the gold stored in a vault on their behalf. Because each purchase is backed by a real asset, the product applies the asset-backing principle to micro-savings.
- **Global Sadaqah** is a Malaysian platform that digitises waqf and sadaqah giving, vetting campaigns and routing donations digitally to verified beneficiaries, improving transparency over traditional cash collection.
- **Digital zakat and waqf** services are now offered by state Islamic religious councils and Islamic banks through apps and internet banking, so Muslims can calculate and pay zakat, or contribute to waqf funds, in a few taps.

These services matter for financial inclusion. Digital channels lower the minimum contribution, widen the donor base and create auditable trails — features that strengthen trust, which is itself a Shariah value (Oseni & Ali, 2019).

### 9.2.4 Takaful Technology and Automated Shariah Screening

**Takaful** is Islamic insurance, in which participants contribute to a common fund used to help members who suffer loss, structured to avoid the gharar and maysir found in conventional insurance. **Takaful tech** applies digital distribution, automated underwriting and claims automation to takaful products — for example, app-based family takaful with online medical declarations, or micro-takaful embedded in e-commerce checkouts. Malaysian takaful operators increasingly partner with fintechs to reach younger, mobile-first customers.

Another behind-the-scenes application is **Shariah screening automation** for equities. To decide whether a listed share is Shariah-compliant, screening methodologies apply qualitative tests (is the core business permissible?) and quantitative financial-ratio tests (for example, how much debt or interest income the company carries). The SC's Shariah Advisory Council publishes its own screening methodology and a regularly updated list of Shariah-compliant securities on Bursa Malaysia. Robo-advisors and brokerage apps then automate these screens: when a portfolio drifts — say a holding becomes non-compliant after a quarterly review — the system flags or rebalances it without human intervention. Automation turns what was once a manual, scholarly exercise into a continuous, real-time control.

## 9.3 Shariah-Compliant Digital Investment and Financing

Beyond banking and crowdfunding, fintech has opened Shariah-compliant investment to ordinary Malaysians through robo-advisors, digitised Islamic contracts and regulated digital-asset trading. This section examines how these work and the governance challenges they raise.

### 9.3.1 Shariah Robo-Advisors: Wahed Invest and BEST Invest

The SC introduced its **Digital Investment Management (DIM)** framework in May 2017 — the first robo-advisory licensing framework in the region — covering automated risk profiling, suitability assessment, portfolio allocation and rebalancing with minimal human supervision (SC, 2017). Among the nine registered DIM operators, two are explicitly Shariah-focused:

- **Wahed Invest**, a global Islamic fintech, offers portfolios built from Shariah-screened exchange-traded funds (ETFs), sukuk and gold, with an in-house Shariah review process.
- **BEST Invest**, operated by BIMB Investment (part of the Bank Islam group), provides Shariah-ESG themed portfolios drawing on one of Malaysia's longest Islamic fund-management track records.

The significance for inclusion is considerable. DIM accounts in Malaysia already exceed one million, with 51% held by investors in their 20s — exactly the demographic that expects Shariah-compliant, low-minimum, mobile-first investing (SC, 2026). A student like Farah from our vignette can start with a few hundred ringgit and obtain a diversified, continuously screened portfolio, something that previously required a unit trust agent and a much larger sum.

### 9.3.2 Digitising Islamic Financial Contracts

Islamic digital products are built from classical contracts, translated into software logic and automated workflows. Table 9.2 summarises the four structures you will meet most often.

**Table 9.2** Major Islamic contracts and their digital applications

| Contract | Structure | Typical digital application |
|---|---|---|
| **Murabahah / Tawarruq** | Cost-plus sale: the financier buys an asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, payable in instalments; tawarruq uses a commodity as the traded asset | Digital bank personal financing; P2P working-capital notes |
| **Wakalah** | Agency: the customer appoints an agent (wakil) to manage funds for a fee | Robo-advisor mandates; takaful operators managing participants' funds |
| **Mudarabah** | Profit-sharing partnership: one party provides capital, the other manages; profits are shared by agreement, losses fall on capital | Islamic investment accounts; some crowdfunding structures |
| **Musharakah** | Joint partnership: all parties contribute capital and share profits and losses | Home financing (diminishing musharakah); venture-style ECF deals |

*Source: Contract descriptions follow Oseni and Ali (2019) and BNM Shariah standards (BNM, 2018a).*

Digitisation changes how these contracts are executed, not what they are. In a digital tawarruq, for example, the commodity trades that must occur before funds are disbursed are executed automatically through an electronic commodity platform in seconds, with each leg time-stamped for Shariah audit. In a DIM wakalah mandate, the algorithm that allocates your portfolio acts as your agent, and its fee is the wakalah fee. The Shariah question shifts from "is the contract valid?" — settled long ago — to "does the code actually perform the contract's steps in the right order, with the right disclosures?" (Oseni & Ali, 2019).

### 9.3.3 Digital Assets as Mal: The SAC's 2020 Resolution

Can a Muslim trade Bitcoin or other digital assets? In 2020, the SC's Shariah Advisory Council issued a landmark resolution recognising **digital assets as *mal* (property)** from a Shariah perspective, and ruling that trading them on SC-registered digital asset exchanges is permissible, subject to conditions (SC, 2024a). Because Malaysia had already prescribed digital currencies and tokens as securities under the *Capital Markets and Services (Prescription of Securities) (Digital Currency and Digital Token) Order 2019*, the resolution gave Islamic investors a regulated pathway: compliant trading is possible, but only on registered exchanges, and crypto remains investment property — not legal tender — in Malaysia.

The resolution illustrates a broader point: Shariah scholarship in Malaysia engages with new technology rather than ignoring it. Classifying digital assets as mal places them within the familiar legal category of property, so established rules on sale, inheritance, zakat and theft can be applied to them.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** "Permissible to trade" does not mean "safe investment". The SAC's resolution addresses Shariah status, not price risk. Digital assets remain highly volatile, and investors bear that risk themselves even on registered exchanges.

### 9.3.4 Challenges: Governing Algorithms and Auditing Smart Contracts

Islamic digital finance inherits all the usual fintech risks — cybersecurity, model risk, data protection — and adds a distinct layer of Shariah-specific challenges (Oseni & Ali, 2019):

1. **Shariah governance of algorithms.** If a robo-advisor's optimisation algorithm selects assets, who certifies that the algorithm's logic — not just its output — is compliant? Shariah committees were designed to review documents and processes, not source code. Institutions increasingly need members or advisors who understand both fiqh muamalat (Islamic commercial jurisprudence) and software.
2. **Standardisation.** Contracts such as tawarruq are accepted in Malaysia but criticised in some other jurisdictions as too close to interest-based lending. A cross-border Islamic fintech must therefore navigate divergent Shariah opinions, raising compliance cost and limiting scalability.
3. **Audit of smart contracts.** If a sukuk is tokenised and profit distributions are executed by self-executing code, an error in the code is an error in the contract. Shariah audit must extend to code review, testing and version control — skills still scarce in the audit profession.
4. **Data and disclosure.** Automated screening depends on timely, accurate company data. If a firm's reported debt level is wrong, the screening output is wrong, and a compliant-labelled portfolio may silently drift out of compliance.

> **🧠 BRAINSTORMER:** Suppose a smart contract automatically distributes sukuk profits, but a coding bug overpays investors by 2% one quarter. From a Shariah and legal standpoint, who bears the loss — the issuer, the programmer or the platform — and how would you design the audit process to catch such bugs before deployment?

## 9.4 Sustainable Finance and Its Role in Digital Finance (Green and Social)

Islamic finance and sustainable finance share a common root: both insist that finance serve real human and social purposes, not merely the multiplication of money. Malaysia has deliberately linked the two agendas, and fintech is the delivery mechanism that makes sustainable finance measurable and scalable.

### 9.4.1 Defining Sustainable, Green and Social Finance — and ESG

**Sustainable finance** refers to financial services and investment decisions that integrate environmental, social and governance (ESG) considerations, so that capital supports long-term, inclusive and climate-resilient economic activity (BNM, 2021). Within it:

- **Green finance** funds activities with environmental benefits — renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transport, pollution control.
- **Social finance** funds activities with social benefits — affordable housing, healthcare, education, financial inclusion and community development.

**ESG** itself is the analytical lens used by investors: *environmental* criteria (emissions, resource use), *social* criteria (labour practices, community impact, product safety) and *governance* criteria (board conduct, transparency, anti-corruption). A company or instrument is assessed on all three pillars, not on financial return alone (SC, 2019).

Note how naturally this vocabulary fits Islamic finance. The maqasid al-Shariah — the higher objectives of Islamic law, including the protection of life, wealth, intellect and posterity — overlap strongly with ESG goals, which is why Malaysia frames sustainability as an extension of, not an alternative to, Islamic finance (BNM, 2018b).

### 9.4.2 From VBI to CCPT: Malaysia's Regulatory Architecture

Malaysia's sustainable-finance rules grew in three deliberate steps.

**Value-based Intermediation (VBI), 2018.** BNM launched VBI together with the Islamic banking industry to re-anchor Islamic finance on its intended outcomes — delivering value to stakeholders, the economy and the community, rather than merely avoiding prohibited elements (BNM, 2018b). VBI encourages Islamic banks to measure and report their impact, for example on entrepreneurship, community empowerment and environmental stewardship, making Islamic banking explicitly outcome-oriented.

**Joint Committee on Climate Change (JC3), 2019.** Recognising that climate risk crosses institutional boundaries, BNM and the SC jointly established JC3 in 2019, co-chaired by the two regulators, to coordinate the financial industry's response to climate change — from risk management practices to capacity building and disclosure (BNM, 2021).

**Climate Change and Principle-based Taxonomy (CCPT), 2021.** BNM's CCPT gives financial institutions a common classification language for economic activities: climate-supporting (C1), watchlist (C2) and climate-affected or harmful categories, based on principles rather than a rigid list (BNM, 2021). A bank can now consistently tag its financing portfolio — a solar farm loan as C1, a coal-related exposure as watchlisted — and manage transition risk. Because the taxonomy is principle-based, institutions exercise documented judgement rather than ticking boxes.

### 9.4.3 Green Sukuk and the SRI Ecosystem

The SC laid the capital-market foundations. Its **Sustainable and Responsible Investment (SRI) framework**, introduced in 2014, allowed funds and sukuk to be designated as SRI products, channelling capital into environmental and social projects (SC, 2014). The **SRI Roadmap (2019)** then set out a five-year strategy to widen the SRI product range, deepen the investor base and build advisory capacity across the Malaysian capital market (SC, 2019).

The flagship result arrived in 2017, when **Tadau Energy** issued the **world's first green sukuk** — an Islamic bond whose proceeds exclusively finance environmentally beneficial projects. The issuance combined two Malaysian strengths in a single instrument: sukuk structuring expertise and an emerging green-finance framework (SC, 2019). A **green sukuk** thus has a dual test to pass: the cash flows must be generated in a Shariah-compliant way, and the use of proceeds must be environmentally certified — reviewed by both Shariah scholars and environmental assessors. The Case Study below examines Tadau's issuance in detail.

### 9.4.4 FinTech Roles in Sustainable Finance — and the Greenwashing Risk

Fintech contributes to sustainable finance in at least four ways:

1. **Green crowdfunding and P2P.** Platforms can earmark campaigns for solar installations, energy-efficiency upgrades or social enterprises, letting retail investors fund small green projects that banks overlook. Shariah-compliant platforms can layer both screens at once.
2. **ESG data analytics.** Start-ups aggregate emissions data, satellite imagery, utility records and disclosures into ESG scores, helping banks apply the CCPT classifications and helping investors compare companies on sustainability rather than marketing claims.
3. **Carbon-tracking apps.** Banking and e-wallet apps increasingly estimate the carbon footprint of a user's spending from transaction data, nudging greener consumption — a direct consumer-facing use of payment analytics.
4. **Tokenised green assets.** Fractional, blockchain-recorded ownership of green assets — such as a share of a solar plant's future revenue — can lower minimum investments and improve traceability of how proceeds are used; BNM's 2025 discussion paper on asset tokenisation examines how such instruments could operate within Malaysian regulation (BNM, 2025).

Every one of these channels creates a temptation toward **greenwashing** — the practice of exaggerating or misrepresenting the environmental or social benefits of a financial product to attract capital (BNM, 2021). A "green" fund that holds coal-adjacent stocks, or a "sustainable" bond with proceeds that drift to general corporate use, destroys the trust on which sustainable finance depends. Regulatory responses include taxonomy-based classification (the CCPT), disclosure standards, and independent external review of use-of-proceeds claims. For students, the working rule is simple: trust the verified data trail, not the label. Digital traceability — from tokenised proceeds tracking to ESG analytics — is precisely the tool that makes verification possible, which is why fintech and credible sustainable finance are natural partners.

> **💡 DID YOU KNOW? (TRIVIA):** When Tadau Energy priced the world's first green sukuk in 2017, the instrument had to satisfy two independent review processes at once — Shariah certification of the sukuk structure and environmental certification of the solar project it financed (SC, 2019).

## Case Study 9.1: Two Malaysian Firsts — Tadau Energy's Green Sukuk and AEON Bank

**Tadau Energy's green sukuk (2017).** In 2017, Tadau Energy, a Malaysian renewable energy company, issued the world's first green sukuk — an RM250 million Sustainable and Responsible Investment (SRI) sukuk — to finance a 50-megawatt solar power plant in Kudat, Sabah. The instrument was issued under the SC's SRI sukuk framework introduced in 2014, and it required two layers of assurance: Shariah review confirming the sukuk's structure and cash flows complied with Islamic principles, and an independent environmental assessment confirming that the proceeds would fund a genuinely green project. The issuance showed that a sukuk could carry both a religious-compliance certificate and a green label without contradiction — the asset-backing of the sukuk matched the physical solar plant, and the social objective of clean energy matched the maqasid of protecting life and posterity. Malaysia thereby created a template that other markets later followed for Islamic green financing (SC, 2014, 2019).

**AEON Bank (2024).** Seven years later, AEON Bank launched on 26 May 2024 as Malaysia's first Islamic digital bank, formed by AEON Financial Service, AEON Credit Service and MoneyLion (BNM, 2026). Operating entirely through a mobile app under BNM's digital bank framework — including the RM3 billion asset limit during its foundational phase — it onboards customers through e-KYC and structures deposits and financing on Shariah-compliant contracts supervised by its Shariah committee. Its target segments include underserved and unbanked Malaysians, linking Islamic finance to the social-finance goal of inclusion: account ownership among the poorest 40% of Malaysians stood at 82.0% in 2024, leaving a meaningful gap that low-cost digital delivery can help close (World Bank, 2025).

## Key Terms

- **Riba**: interest or usury; any guaranteed return on money lent, prohibited in Islamic finance because returns must be earned through real trade or investment.
- **Gharar**: excessive uncertainty or ambiguity in a contract's subject matter, price or obligations, which renders the contract invalid under Shariah.
- **Maysir**: gambling or purely speculative gain where one party profits from another's loss by chance; prohibited under Shariah.
- **Islamic digital finance**: the use of financial technologies to deliver products and services that comply with Shariah principles in both contract and operation.
- **Shariah Advisory Council (SAC)**: the apex authority under the Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009 for ascertaining Islamic law for Islamic financial business in Malaysia.
- **Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 (IFSA)**: the Malaysian statute regulating Islamic financial institutions and imposing a statutory duty of continuous Shariah compliance.
- **Murabahah**: a cost-plus sale contract in which the financier buys an asset and resells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, payable in instalments.
- **Tawarruq**: monetisation via commodity murabahah; buying a commodity on deferred terms and selling it for cash to obtain liquidity.
- **Wakalah**: an agency contract in which a principal appoints an agent to act on their behalf, typically for a fee; used in robo-advisory and takaful.
- **Mudarabah**: a profit-sharing partnership where one party supplies capital and the other supplies management; losses fall on the capital provider.
- **Musharakah**: a joint partnership in which all parties contribute capital and share profits and losses by agreed ratios.
- **Takaful**: Islamic insurance based on mutual contributions to a common fund that compensates members who suffer loss, avoiding gharar and maysir.
- **Sukuk**: Shariah-compliant investment certificates representing ownership of underlying assets or ventures, generating returns from those assets rather than interest.
- **Green sukuk**: a sukuk whose proceeds are used exclusively to finance environmentally beneficial projects, passing both Shariah and environmental review.
- **Value-based Intermediation (VBI)**: BNM's 2018 initiative with the Islamic banking industry to deliver measurable value to stakeholders, the economy and the community.
- **Environmental, social and governance (ESG)**: the three-pillar framework for assessing the sustainability and ethical impact of a company or investment.
- **Greenwashing**: exaggerating or misrepresenting the environmental or social benefits of a financial product or activity to attract capital.
- **Digital Investment Management (DIM)**: the SC's 2017 licensing framework for robo-advisors providing automated portfolio management with minimal human supervision.

## Problem-Solving Corner

### Problem 1: Conventional Loan vs Murabahah Cost-Plus Financing

Encik Rahman needs RM50,000 to buy machinery for his workshop, repayable over 3 years in equal monthly instalments. He compares two offers:

- **Offer A (conventional loan):** borrow RM50,000 at a flat interest rate of 8% per annum for 3 years.
- **Offer B (murabahah):** the Islamic bank buys the machinery for RM50,000 and sells it to Encik Rahman at cost plus a profit of 8% per annum on the cost price for 3 years, payable in equal monthly instalments.

**(a)** Compute the total amount payable and the monthly instalment under each offer.
**(b)** Identify two differences between the offers that matter even though the cash flows look identical.

#### Solution to Problem 1

**(a) Offer A — conventional loan.**

- Interest = RM50,000 × 8% × 3 years = **RM12,000**
- Total payable = RM50,000 + RM12,000 = **RM62,000**
- Monthly instalment = RM62,000 ÷ 36 = **RM1,722.22** (rounded to sen)

**Offer B — murabahah.**

- Profit = RM50,000 × 8% × 3 years = **RM12,000**
- Murabahah selling price = RM50,000 + RM12,000 = **RM62,000**
- Monthly instalment = RM62,000 ÷ 36 = **RM1,722.22**

The economic cash flows are identical — a reminder that Shariah compliance is about *how* the return is generated, not about being cheaper.

**(b) Key differences.**

1. **Ownership and asset risk.** Under murabahah, the bank must genuinely own the machinery before selling it, bearing ownership risk (however briefly) and guaranteeing valid title; under the loan, the bank never owns the asset — it only lends money. This is the asset-backing principle in action.
2. **Nature of the obligation and default treatment.** The RM62,000 in Offer B is a *sale price*, a debt fixed at the moment of sale; it cannot be increased by charging "interest on late interest" (compounding riba). Late-payment charges on Islamic financing are restricted (for example, taʿwidh compensation limited to actual loss), whereas a conventional loan can contractually capitalise penalty interest. The legal character of the customer's obligation therefore differs even though the schedule looks the same.

### Problem 2: Screening a Portfolio for Shariah Compliance

You are a junior analyst at a Shariah robo-advisor. Using the **illustrative screening thresholds** commonly taught in introductory Islamic finance — a company's total conventional debt must be **below 30%** of its market capitalisation, and its income from non-compliant activities (interest, alcohol, gambling, conventional financial services) must be **below 5%** of total income — screen the three companies below. (Note: these thresholds are teaching illustrations; regulators and index providers, including the SC's Shariah Advisory Council, publish their own detailed benchmarks.)

| Company | Core business | Debt / market cap | Non-compliant income / total income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tekun Bhd | Halal food manufacturing | 28% | 3% |
| MegaBuild Bhd | Construction | 45% | 1% |
| Serantau Bhd | Logistics | 25% | 6.5% |

**(a)** Determine which companies pass the screen, showing each test.
**(b)** Your robo-advisor currently holds all three. State what action the portfolio algorithm should take.

#### Solution to Problem 2

**(a) Screening results.**

- **Tekun Bhd:** qualitative test — halal food manufacturing is a permissible business. Debt ratio 28% < 30% (pass). Non-compliant income 3% < 5% (pass). **Overall: PASSES** — Shariah-compliant.
- **MegaBuild Bhd:** construction is permissible in principle, but debt ratio 45% > 30% (**fails the financial-ratio test**). Non-compliant income 1% < 5% (passes). **Overall: FAILS** — excluded because one failed test is enough.
- **Serantau Bhd:** logistics is permissible; debt ratio 25% < 30% (passes); but non-compliant income 6.5% > 5% (**fails the income test**). **Overall: FAILS** — excluded.

**(b) Portfolio action.** The algorithm should **divest MegaBuild Bhd and Serantau Bhd**, retaining only Tekun Bhd, and reallocate the proceeds to other compliant securities in the model portfolio. In practice, it would also calculate a **dividend purification** amount: the small non-compliant portion of income earned while holding borderline stocks is donated to charity rather than kept by the investor — an automated feature in Shariah robo-advisors such as Wahed Invest. Finally, screening is continuous: the system re-checks ratios each reporting period, because a pass today can become a fail after the next financial statement.
