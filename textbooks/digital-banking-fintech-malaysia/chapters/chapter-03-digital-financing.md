## Opening Vignette

Aina Damia runs *Dapur Aina*, a small frozen curry-puff business in Klang, Selangor. For three years she has supplied school cooperatives and neighbourhood grocers, buying ingredients with her own savings. One morning, a supermarket chain offers her a contract to supply 40 outlets — but she must first buy a second freezer, a packaging machine and larger stocks of flour and potatoes. She needs RM40,000 within a month.

Her bank asks for audited accounts, collateral and a guarantor. Aina has none of these; her accounts are simple spreadsheets, and the shop lot is rented. A friend mentions a peer-to-peer financing platform registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia. Aina applies online, uploads six months of bank statements and her purchase order, and receives a decision in days. Investors on the platform fund her campaign, and she repays the financing over six months as the supermarket pays her invoices.

Aina's story shows why digital financing exists — and why it matters to thousands of Malaysian micro, small and medium enterprises. This chapter explains how it works, what it costs, and what can go wrong.

## 3.1 Capital Raising and Digital Financing

Every business needs capital. Money is needed to start a business (seed capital), to buy equipment (capital expenditure) and to pay for daily running costs such as wages, rent and raw materials (working capital). Traditionally, Malaysian businesses raised this money from their own savings, from family and friends, from bank loans or — for larger companies — from issuing shares and bonds. This section explains why that traditional system leaves many small businesses behind, and how digital channels are filling the gap.

### 3.1.1 The Traditional SME Financing Gap

**Digital financing** refers to the provision of financial services — particularly credit and capital raising — through digital technologies, platforms and channels rather than through traditional branch-based processes (Arner et al., 2016; Puschmann, 2017). It matters because the traditional system has a persistent gap.

Micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs) form the backbone of the Malaysian economy. They employ millions of Malaysians and include everything from roadside food stalls and online sellers to small manufacturers and professional firms. Yet many of them cannot borrow as much as they need, when they need it. The difference between the financing that businesses demand and the financing that traditional institutions are willing to supply is known as the **financing gap**.

The financing gap exists on both sides of the market. On the demand side, micro-SMEs typically want small loans — sometimes only RM10,000 to RM50,000 — for short periods, often urgently. On the supply side, banks find such loans unattractive: the administrative cost of assessing a RM30,000 loan is almost the same as assessing a RM3 million loan, but the profit is a tiny fraction. Thakor (2020) notes that this mismatch between what small borrowers need and what banks find profitable is one of the main reasons technology-based lenders emerged around the world.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** Financing comes in two basic forms. *Debt* financing is borrowed money that must be repaid with interest or profit; the lender does not own any part of the business. *Equity* financing means selling a share of ownership; there is no repayment, but the investor shares in future profits and losses. P2P financing is debt; equity crowdfunding is equity.

### 3.1.2 Why Banks Underserve Micro-SMEs

Understanding the financing gap requires understanding how banks make lending decisions. Four factors explain why banks underserve micro-SMEs.

**1. Information asymmetry.** **Information asymmetry** exists when one party in a transaction has more or better information than the other party (Thakor, 2020). A small business owner knows far more about the real condition of the business than the bank does. Banks protect themselves by demanding audited financial statements, several years of track record and credit histories. Many micro-SMEs cannot produce these documents, not because they are dishonest, but because they are small and informal. Faced with uncertainty, the bank either declines the application or charges a high price.

**2. Collateral requirements.** Banks typically secure their loans against assets such as property or fixed deposits. Micro-entrepreneurs often rent their premises and have few assets to pledge, so they fail this test even when their businesses are profitable.

**3. High cost-to-serve.** Manual underwriting — visiting the business, verifying documents, assessing character — costs time and money. For small loans, these fixed costs make the business uneconomic for banks.

**4. Speed and rigidity.** Bank approval processes can take weeks. A small trader who must pay a supplier within days to secure a festive-season order cannot wait. The result is that many micro-SMEs rely on personal savings, borrow from family, use supplier credit or — in the worst cases — turn to unlicensed moneylenders.

### 3.1.3 Digital Channels and Malaysia's Alternative Financing Market

Digital financing platforms attack each of these four problems. Online onboarding replaces branch visits; electronic know-your-customer (e-KYC) processes verify identity remotely; automated credit assessment lowers the cost of evaluating small loans; and platform marketplaces allow many small funders to combine their money to meet one borrower's needs (Gomber et al., 2018).

Malaysia's alternative financing market is supervised by the Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) and consists mainly of two segments: peer-to-peer (P2P) financing and equity crowdfunding (ECF). The scale is now significant. By the end of 2025, P2P financing platforms had channelled a cumulative RM11.32 billion to Malaysian businesses through 154,283 campaigns, while ECF platforms had raised a cumulative RM917.51 million across 445 campaigns (Securities Commission Malaysia [SC], 2026). These flows complement, rather than replace, the banking system, and they align with the national agenda in the *Financial Sector Blueprint 2022–2026*, which champions a more digital and inclusive financial system (Bank Negara Malaysia [BNM], 2022).

Table 3.1 summarises how the two digital channels compare with traditional bank financing.

**Table 3.1: Traditional bank financing versus P2P financing versus equity crowdfunding**

| Dimension | Bank financing | P2P financing | Equity crowdfunding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instrument | Loan or overdraft | Investment note (debt) | Shares (equity) |
| Source of funds | Bank's balance sheet | Many individual/institutional investors | Many individual/institutional investors |
| Repayment obligation | Yes, with interest/profit | Yes, with agreed returns | No; investors share profits and losses |
| Collateral | Commonly required | Usually unsecured | Not applicable |

### 3.1.4 The Regulatory Perimeter: The SC and the CMSA 2007

In Malaysia, responsibility for financial regulation is divided. Bank Negara Malaysia regulates banks, payment systems, e-money and money services, while the Securities Commission Malaysia regulates capital-market fintech — including P2P financing, equity crowdfunding, digital asset exchanges and digital investment management (SC, 2026).

The legal foundation is the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007 (Act 671), which governs securities and capital-market activities. Because investment notes and ECF shares are capital-market products, the platforms that offer them must be registered with the SC as **Recognized Market Operators (RMOs)** — that is, operators of electronic platforms approved to bring together issuers and investors under the *Guidelines on Recognized Markets* (SC, 2024). The Guidelines set out obligations on governance, due diligence over issuers, disclosure to investors, handling of client monies and fair treatment.

Malaysia moved early. It was the first country in ASEAN to regulate equity crowdfunding, with the SC's framework announced in 2015, and it was among the first in the region to regulate P2P financing in 2016 (SC, 2026). This early, rules-based approach gave legitimate operators certainty and gave investors a regulated environment in which to participate.

> **🇲🇾 MALAYSIAN SPOTLIGHT:** The Securities Commission Malaysia, established in 1993, has become one of Asia's most active regulators of alternative finance. Its decision to license P2P and ECF platforms in 2015–2016 — years before many neighbours — helped build a market that by 2025 had supplied more than RM12 billion in cumulative alternative financing to Malaysian MSMEs, much of it to businesses too small for conventional bank credit (SC, 2026).

## 3.2 Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Financing

P2P financing is the larger of Malaysia's two alternative-financing segments and the one most relevant to ordinary small businesses. This section defines the concept, explains how investment notes work, shows how platforms assess credit using alternative data, and presents the Malaysian market in numbers.

### 3.2.1 What Is P2P Financing?

**Peer-to-peer (P2P) financing** is the practice of raising debt funding from a large number of investors through an online platform that matches funders directly with borrowers, without the funding passing through a traditional financial intermediary such as a bank (Thakor, 2020; Lee & Shin, 2018). In Malaysia the borrowers are businesses, so the SC uses the term "P2P financing" rather than "P2P lending", and the products are structured as securities called investment notes (SC, 2024).

Three parties take part in every transaction:

- The **issuer** — the business that raises funds by issuing an investment note and promises to repay it with returns.
- The **investors** — individuals or institutions who subscribe to the note in amounts of their choosing and receive the repayments.
- The **platform operator** — the RMO that vets issuers, hosts the campaign, handles the money and administers repayments.

The platform is a marketplace, not a bank. It does not lend its own money; it earns fees for arranging the transaction. This distinction is important for understanding both the costs (Section 3.4) and the risks (Section 3.5) of P2P financing.

> **💡 DID YOU KNOW? (TRIVIA):** Malaysia was the first country in ASEAN to put equity crowdfunding under a formal regulatory framework (2015) and among the first in the region to regulate P2P financing (2016). Within a decade, those early frameworks had channelled RM11.32 billion in P2P funding to Malaysian businesses (SC, 2026).

### 3.2.2 How Investment Notes Work: Issuers and Investors

An **investment note** is a security issued through a registered P2P platform that evidences the issuer's obligation to repay investors the principal amount together with agreed returns over a fixed period, known as the tenor (SC, 2024). Tenors in the market commonly run from a few months to a few years, and returns may be structured on conventional (interest-based) or Shariah-compliant (profit-based) terms.

The issuer's journey typically follows six steps:

1. **Application.** The business applies online, submitting its registration documents (for example, Suruhanjaya Syarikat Malaysia or SSM records), bank statements, financial information and details of the funding purpose.
2. **Credit assessment.** The platform verifies the information, analyses the business's ability to repay and assigns an internal risk grade, which determines the rate of return offered to investors.
3. **Listing.** The approved note is listed on the platform with its amount, tenor, rate and purpose. Investors browse listings and decide how much to subscribe.
4. **Funding.** When subscriptions reach the target, the campaign closes. If the target is not met within the listing period, the campaign is cancelled and monies are returned to investors.
5. **Disbursement.** The platform deducts its fees and releases the net proceeds to the issuer.
6. **Repayment.** The issuer repays according to the schedule — either in regular instalments of principal and returns, or as a single "bullet" repayment at maturity. The platform collects and distributes the money to investors.

For investors, the attraction is access to a fixed-income style product with disclosed rates and the ability to spread money across many notes. A investor with RM10,000 can place RM500 into twenty different issuers instead of betting everything on one. This practice of spreading exposure across many borrowers is called **diversification**, and it is the single most important risk-management habit for P2P investors.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** An investment note is a *security*, not a bank deposit. It is not protected by deposit insurance, and the advertised rate of return is a promise, not a guarantee. If the issuer fails to pay, investors can lose part or all of their money.

### 3.2.3 Credit Assessment with Alternative Data

How can a platform lend to a business with no audited accounts and no collateral? The answer is technology plus data. **Credit scoring** is the use of statistical models to estimate the probability that a borrower will repay a debt, usually expressed as a score or grade (Thakor, 2020). Traditional scoring depends heavily on formal credit-bureau records and financial statements. Digital platforms supplement these with **alternative data** — non-traditional digital footprints such as bank-account transaction flows, e-payment and e-wallet records, online marketplace sales histories, invoices and utility payment records (Gomber et al., 2018).

Alternative data helps in three ways. First, it is *timely*: a platform can observe the business's actual cash receipts last month, not last year's audited figures. Second, it is *granular*: daily inflows and outflows reveal seasonality and stress that annual accounts hide. Third, it is *cheap to process*: automated analysis reduces the cost of underwriting small loans, directly attacking the cost-to-serve problem described in Section 3.1.2.

For a food manufacturer, for instance, the platform might examine six months of bank statements to confirm steady sales deposits, review a confirmed purchase order from a supermarket to verify the purpose of the financing, and check public records for litigation or winding-up petitions. The result is a risk grade that prices the note: stronger businesses pay lower returns; weaker ones pay more, or are declined. Section 3.5 returns to the limitations and risks of data-driven credit assessment.

## 3.3 Types of Crowdfunding and Equity Crowdfunding

P2P financing is, in fact, one member of a larger family: crowdfunding. This section defines the four main types of crowdfunding, then focuses on equity crowdfunding (ECF), the segment in which investors become part-owners of the businesses they back.

### 3.3.1 Four Types of Crowdfunding

**Crowdfunding** is the practice of raising small amounts of money from a large number of people — "the crowd" — through an online platform, typically to fund a project, cause or business (Chishti & Barberis, 2016; Lee & Shin, 2018). What the contributor receives in return defines the type of crowdfunding, as summarised in Table 3.3.

**Table 3.3: The four main types of crowdfunding**

| Type | What the backer receives | Typical use | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Donation-based | Nothing material; the satisfaction of giving | Charity, community, disaster relief | Funding a mosque's flood-relief appeal |
| Reward-based | A product, service or token of appreciation | Creative projects, product pre-orders | Pre-ordering a gadget before it is manufactured |
| Debt-based (P2P) | Repayment of principal with returns | Business working capital | An investment note for a food manufacturer |
| Equity-based (ECF) | Shares in the company | Early-stage business growth | Buying shares in a tech startup |

Donation and reward crowdfunding are generally outside securities regulation because backers do not receive financial instruments. Debt and equity crowdfunding involve securities and therefore fall within the SC's regulatory perimeter in Malaysia (SC, 2024). Islamic variants exist across the family: Malaysian platforms operate Shariah-compliant debt and equity crowdfunding, and donation-based crowdfunding aligns naturally with *sadaqah* and *waqf* giving (Oseni & Ali, 2019).

### 3.3.2 Equity Crowdfunding: Concept and Investor Returns

**Equity crowdfunding (ECF)** is a form of crowdfunding in which investors subscribe for shares in a company through an online platform, becoming part-owners who share in the company's future profits and losses (SC, 2024). Unlike a P2P investor, an ECF investor is *not* promised repayment. Returns come only if the company prospers — through dividends, a trade sale of the company, a buy-back of shares or, rarely, a stock-exchange listing.

Two valuation concepts drive every ECF deal. The **pre-money valuation** is the value agreed for the company *before* the new money arrives; the **post-money valuation** is the pre-money valuation *plus* the amount raised. The percentage of the company that new investors receive equals the amount raised divided by the post-money valuation. The corresponding reduction in existing shareholders' ownership percentage is called **dilution** — founders still hold the same number of shares, but those shares represent a smaller slice of a larger company. The worked example in the Problem-Solving Corner shows the arithmetic in detail.

ECF suits businesses that are young, fast-growing and hungry for cash that debt cannot safely carry. A startup with no stable revenue cannot promise monthly repayments, but it can offer investors a share of a potentially valuable future. The trade-off is risk: many early-stage companies fail, and ECF shares are *illiquid* — there is usually no ready market on which to sell them, so investors must be prepared to hold for years or lose the entire investment.

Malaysia was the first country in ASEAN to regulate equity crowdfunding, announcing the SC framework in 2015, with campaigns beginning in 2016 (SC, 2026). A decade later, the market remains much smaller than P2P financing — reflecting the smaller population of businesses suited to equity — but it is growing quickly. ## 3.4 Platform Fees and Financing Costs

Digital financing is convenient, but it is not free. Platforms charge fees to run their marketplaces, and investors demand returns for bearing risk. This section maps the typical fee structures, shows how to compute the *effective* cost of P2P financing, and explains the different cost logic of equity crowdfunding.

### 3.4.1 Typical Fee Structures on Digital Financing Platforms

Platforms earn revenue by charging issuers, investors or both. The exact amounts vary between operators and are disclosed on each platform, as required by the disclosure obligations in the *Guidelines on Recognized Markets* (SC, 2024). Table 3.5 lists the fee types a student is likely to encounter.

**Table 3.5: Common fee types on digital financing platforms**

| Fee type | Who pays | When charged | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listing / **origination fee** | Issuer | On successful funding (often deducted from proceeds) | Covers application processing, credit assessment and documentation |
| Success fee | Issuer | Only if the campaign succeeds | Platform's main revenue for arranging the deal |
| Servicing / administration fee | Issuer or investor | During the tenor (e.g., monthly) | Covers collection and distribution of repayments |
| Investor fee | Investor | On returns received | Platform's share for providing the marketplace |
| Late fee / penalty | Issuer | On overdue payments | Compensates for delay; may be shared with investors |
| Early-settlement fee | Issuer (some platforms) | If the note is repaid early | Compensates investors/platform for lost returns |

An **origination fee** is a charge levied by a platform for processing, assessing and arranging a financing facility, usually expressed as a percentage of the amount raised and deducted from the proceeds (SC, 2024). Two practical points follow. First, because the fee is deducted upfront, the issuer receives *less* cash than the face amount of the note — but must repay the *full* face amount. Second, fees stack: a note may carry an origination fee, servicing fees *and* late fees, so the true cost exceeds the headline rate of return.

> **📌 QUICK NOTE:** The advertised rate on an investment note (say, 10% per annum) is the *investor's* return, not the *issuer's* cost. The issuer's true cost is higher once origination and servicing fees are included, and it must be measured against the net cash actually received.

### 3.4.2 The Effective Cost of P2P Financing versus Bank Loans

To compare financing options fairly, businesses should compute the **effective annual cost** — the total cost of financing (returns plus all fees) expressed as an annual rate on the net funds actually received by the borrower (Thakor, 2020). A simple illustration makes the idea concrete (a fuller version appears in the Problem-Solving Corner):

- An SME issues a six-month investment note for RM150,000 at 10% per annum (simple), with a 2% origination fee.
- Returns payable at maturity: RM150,000 × 10% × 6/12 = RM7,500.
- Origination fee: RM150,000 × 2% = RM3,000, deducted upfront, so the SME receives RM147,000.
- Total six-month cost: RM7,500 + RM3,000 = RM10,500, on RM147,000 actually received — a six-month cost of about 7.14%, or roughly 14.3% annualised.

How does this compare with a bank loan? Secured bank financing is usually *cheaper in rate terms*, because banks fund themselves with low-cost deposits. P2P financing is usually *more expensive*, because investors bear unsecured credit risk and platforms must cover their costs. But the comparison is not only about price. P2P financing typically offers faster approval, no collateral requirement, smaller ticket sizes and shorter tenors — precisely the features micro-SMEs need. For a business facing a time-sensitive opportunity, a more expensive facility that arrives in a week can be worth more than a cheaper one that arrives in three months, or never. The rational borrower compares *availability, speed and cost* together (Thakor, 2020).

### 3.4.3 The Cost of Equity Crowdfunding

ECF has a different cost logic, because there is no debt to repay. Instead, an ECF issuer faces three kinds of cost:

1. **Cash fees.** Platforms typically charge a success fee on the amount raised, payable only if the campaign completes; some also charge listing or administrative fees. Legal, due-diligence and marketing expenses add to the bill, because a public campaign requires a polished pitch, financial disclosures and often a video and media outreach.
2. **Time and disclosure costs.** Founders must prepare detailed information for public scrutiny and manage relations with dozens or hundreds of new shareholders after the campaign.
3. **Dilution.** The largest economic "cost" is the ownership given up. Selling 20% of the company means the founders' claim on future profits falls by one-fifth — permanently, unless shares are later bought back.

The absence of repayment obligations, however, is a genuine benefit: an ECF-funded company keeps all its cash for growth and owes investors nothing if the plan fails. For high-risk, high-growth ventures, equity is often the *only* sustainable financing — no prudent lender will fund a firm that cannot service debt. Students should therefore resist the question "which is cheaper?" and ask instead "which instrument matches the firm's cash-flow profile and risk?".

## 3.5 Risks, Financial Inclusion and Regulatory Considerations

Digital financing creates real opportunities, but it also creates new ways to lose money. This final section analyses the main risks, explains how the SC's framework protects investors, introduces Shariah-compliant options, and assesses what digital financing has — and has not — achieved for financial inclusion in Malaysia.

### 3.5.1 Default Risk, Platform Failure and Information Asymmetry

**Default risk** (credit risk) is the risk that a borrower will fail to make required payments, causing lenders or investors to lose interest, profit or principal (Thakor, 2020). Default risk is the central risk of P2P financing: notes are usually unsecured, issuers are small businesses with fragile cash flows, and there is no deposit insurance. Mitigants include the platform's credit assessment, diversification across many notes, and — on some platforms — provision funds or security arrangements. None of these eliminates the risk.

**Platform failure** is a second, distinct risk. Investors and issuers depend on the platform to hold monies safely, collect repayments and enforce the notes. If an operator becomes insolvent or exits the market, servicing of outstanding notes could be disrupted even if the underlying borrowers remain healthy. The *Guidelines on Recognized Markets* address this by imposing obligations on governance, segregation of client monies and orderly conduct of the platform's business (SC, 2024), but investors should still prefer registered operators and read the platform's arrangements for business continuity.

Third, **information asymmetry** does not disappear online — it changes shape. Platforms know more about their credit models than investors do; issuers know more about their true condition than the platform does; and polished campaign pages can flatter weak businesses (Zetzsche et al., 2017). Related dangers include issuers misstating their finances, campaigns raising money for fictitious purposes, and outright **fraud** by fake platforms. The first line of defence is simple: deal only with operators on the SC's register of recognized market operators, and treat any unsolicited "guaranteed high return" offer as a red flag.

### 3.5.2 Investor Protection under the Recognized Market Framework

The SC's approach combines gatekeeping, disclosure and investment limits:

- **Gatekeeping.** Platforms must be registered as RMOs and satisfy fit-and-proper, governance and systems requirements. Operators are obliged to conduct due diligence on issuers before listing their campaigns (SC, 2024).
- **Disclosure.** Issuers must provide prescribed information about their business, financial condition and the purpose of the fundraising, enabling investors to make informed decisions (SC, 2024).
- **Investment limits.** To contain the damage any one failure can do, the Guidelines prescribe investment limits for retail investors — caps on how much an individual may commit to a single issuer and in aggregate over a period — while sophisticated investors, who are deemed better able to assess and absorb risk, enjoy wider access (SC, 2024).

This architecture illustrates a permanent regulatory trade-off. Rules that are too lax invite scandals that destroy public trust; rules that are too strict smother a young industry that serves borrowers the banks ignore. Malaysia's answer has been to allow experimentation *inside* a regulated perimeter — licensing real operators, mandating disclosure and capping retail exposure — rather than banning the activity or leaving it unregulated (Zetzsche et al., 2017).

### 3.5.3 Shariah-Compliant Options

Malaysia's position as a global leader in Islamic finance extends into alternative financing. **Shariah-compliant** financing is financing structured in accordance with Islamic law — notably the prohibition of *riba* (interest) and excessive uncertainty — using contracts such as cost-plus sale (*murabahah*/*tawarruq*) or agency (*wakalah*) instead of conventional interest-bearing loans (Oseni & Ali, 2019).

The supply of Shariah-compliant digital financing is substantial and growing. In 2025, 26% of all funds raised on Malaysian P2P platforms were Shariah-compliant, and operators such as microLEAP specialise in Shariah-compliant notes, while Ethis operates Islamic P2P and ECF offerings (SC, 2026; DinarStandard & Elipses, 2026). Shariah-compliant campaigns remain a smaller share of ECF — 4.1% of campaigns — suggesting room for growth (SC, 2026). Globally, Malaysia ranks among the top Islamic-fintech ecosystems, within a market estimated at about US$198 billion in transaction volume and projected to reach US$341 billion by 2029 (DinarStandard & Elipses, 2026). For Muslim investors and issuers, these options make it possible to participate in digital financing without compromising religious principles; for platforms, they widen the investor base.

### 3.5.4 Financial Inclusion: MSMEs and B40 Entrepreneurs

**Financial inclusion** means that individuals and businesses have access to, and actually use, affordable and suitable financial products and services (World Bank, 2025). Malaysia performs well on basic access — 88.7% of adults owned a financial account in 2024, with a gender gap below one percentage point — but access to *credit* is weaker for poorer households: account ownership among the poorest 40% stood at 82.0% in 2024, and owning an account does not guarantee the ability to borrow (World Bank, 2025).

Digital financing helps close this credit gap in several ways. Small ticket sizes (62% of 2025 P2P campaigns raised RM50,000 or less) match the needs of micro-businesses; unsecured notes remove the collateral barrier; alternative-data credit scoring serves thin-file applicants; and fully digital journeys reduce travel and paperwork for rural entrepreneurs (SC, 2026). The issuer base confirms the inclusive character of the market: 99% of issuers are non-technology businesses, and 54% come from Selangor and Kuala Lumpur, meaning the other 46% are spread across the rest of the country (SC, 2026). For entrepreneurs in the B40 (bottom 40% income) group — a food-stall operator formalising her business, a village craftsman buying equipment — a successful small campaign can be the first rung on the ladder of formal credit, building a repayment record that unlocks larger facilities later.

Two cautions complete the picture. First, digital financing presupposes digital access and literacy; entrepreneurs without smartphones, connectivity or the skills to prepare an online application may be left further behind. Second, inclusion must not become *over-indebtedness*: easy credit that funds consumption rather than productive investment can harm the very households it is meant to help. Sustainable inclusion pairs access with financial education — which is precisely why you are studying this chapter.

## Case Study 3.1: Sri Cahaya Food Products Sdn. Bhd. — Raising RM150,000 Working Capital through a P2P Investment Note

*Note: the company and figures in this case are fictional and for illustration only; fee and rate assumptions are stated explicitly.*

Sri Cahaya Food Products Sdn. Bhd. is a halal food manufacturer in Shah Alam, Selangor, producing bottled sambal and cooking sauces. The company, owned by Encik Rahman Hassan, has operated for eight years, employs 22 workers and sells through grocers and online marketplaces. In March, a national supermarket chain offers Sri Cahaya a RM480,000 contract to supply a festive-season promotion, with delivery in five months and payment within 60 days of delivery.

**The problem.** Fulfilling the order requires RM150,000 upfront for chillies, glass bottles, labels and seasonal workers. Sri Cahaya's bank declines an overdraft: the company rents its factory, has no property to pledge, and its thin credit file produces a weak bureau score. The contract will be lost without alternative funding.

**The application.** Encik Rahman applies to an SC-registered P2P financing platform. He uploads the company's SSM registration, six months of business bank statements, the latest management accounts, and the supermarket's signed purchase order.

**Credit scoring.** The platform verifies the documents and analyses alternative data: steady monthly sales deposits in the bank statements, growing online-marketplace sales, and the creditworthiness of the supermarket behind the purchase order. Sri Cahaya receives a risk grade consistent with a sound, established business, and the platform approves an investment note of RM150,000 for a six-month tenor at an illustrative 10% per annum (simple), repayable in a single bullet payment at maturity when the supermarket's invoice is settled.

**Funding.** The note is listed, and over several days more than 90 investors subscribe amounts of their choosing until the RM150,000 target is filled. The platform deducts its 2% origination fee (RM3,000) and disburses RM147,000 to Sri Cahaya.

**Repayment.** Production runs through Ramadan; delivery is made on schedule, and the supermarket pays in month five. At month six, Sri Cahaya repays RM157,500 — the RM150,000 principal plus RM7,500 in returns — which the platform distributes to investors. The contract yields a healthy gross margin even after the total financing cost of RM10,500, and Sri Cahaya gains two assets beyond cash: a national retail customer and a successful repayment record that improves its standing for future financing.

## Chapter Summary

- **3.1:** Banks underserve micro-SMEs because of information asymmetry, collateral requirements, high cost-to-serve and slow approval processes.
- **3.1:** In Malaysia, the Securities Commission Malaysia regulates P2P financing and equity crowdfunding under the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007, with platforms registered as Recognized Market Operators under the *Guidelines on Recognized Markets*.
- **3.2:** P2P financing raises debt from many investors via an online platform; businesses issue investment notes, which are securities promising repayment of principal with agreed returns.
- **3.2:** Platforms assess credit using traditional records plus alternative data such as bank transaction flows, e-payment records and marketplace sales, enabling them to serve thin-file SMEs.
- **3.2:** Malaysia's P2P market has raised RM11.32 billion cumulatively through 154,283 campaigns; in 2025 it raised RM2.83 billion, with 62% of campaigns at RM50,000 or less and 98% funding working capital (SC, 2026).
- **3.3:** Crowdfunding comes in four types — donation, reward, debt and equity; equity crowdfunding gives investors shares, with returns depending entirely on the company's success.
- **3.3:** Malaysia was the first ASEAN country to regulate ECF (2015); the market has raised RM917.51 million cumulatively across 445 campaigns, dominated by seed and Series A stage companies (SC, 2026).
- **3.4:** Platforms charge listing/origination, success, servicing, investor and late fees; because fees are deducted upfront, the issuer's effective annual cost exceeds the headline rate on the note.
- **3.5:** Key risks are default, platform failure, information asymmetry and fraud; the SC mitigates them through RMO registration, disclosure duties and retail investment limits.
- **3.5:** Digital financing advances financial inclusion for MSMEs and B40 entrepreneurs — 26% of P2P funds in 2025 were Shariah-compliant — but digital literacy gaps and over-indebtedness remain concerns.

## Key Terms

- **Digital financing**: the provision of financial services, especially credit and capital raising, through digital technologies and platforms rather than traditional branch-based processes.
- **Peer-to-peer (P2P) financing**: raising debt funding from many investors through an online platform that matches funders directly with business borrowers, bypassing banks as intermediaries.
- **Investment note**: a security issued through a registered P2P platform evidencing the issuer's obligation to repay investors principal plus agreed returns over a fixed tenor.
- **Issuer**: the business that raises funds on a P2P or ECF platform by issuing investment notes or shares, and bears the obligation to repay or to share ownership.
- **Recognized Market Operator (RMO)**: an electronic platform operator registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia to run a market — such as P2P, ECF or digital asset trading — under the Guidelines on Recognized Markets.
- **Crowdfunding**: raising small amounts of money from a large number of people through an online platform to fund a project, cause or business.
- **Equity crowdfunding (ECF)**: a form of crowdfunding in which investors subscribe for shares in a company through an online platform, becoming part-owners who share its profits and losses.
- **Origination fee**: a charge levied by a platform for processing, assessing and arranging a financing facility, usually a percentage of the amount raised, deducted from the proceeds.
- **Effective annual cost**: the total cost of financing, including returns and all fees, expressed as an annual rate on the net funds actually received by the borrower.
- **Credit scoring**: the use of statistical models to estimate the probability that a borrower will repay a debt, typically summarised as a score or risk grade.
- **Alternative data**: non-traditional digital information — such as bank transaction flows, e-payment records and online sales — used to assess creditworthiness when formal records are thin.
- **Information asymmetry**: a situation in which one party to a transaction holds more or better information than the other, complicating lending and investment decisions.
- **Default risk**: the risk that a borrower fails to make required payments, causing lenders or investors to lose returns, principal or both.
- **Pre-money valuation**: the agreed value of a company immediately before new investment money is added in a funding round.
- **Post-money valuation**: a company's value immediately after a funding round, equal to the pre-money valuation plus the amount of new capital raised.
- **Dilution**: the reduction in existing shareholders' ownership percentage when new shares are issued to new investors, even though their share count is unchanged.
- **Financial inclusion**: the condition in which individuals and businesses can access and use affordable, suitable financial products and services.
- **Shariah-compliant**: structured in accordance with Islamic law, avoiding prohibited elements such as riba (interest) through contracts like murabahah, tawarruq or wakalah.

**Part B (model answers)**

**1.** Two reasons are (i) *information asymmetry*: banks demand audited accounts and credit histories that informal micro-SMEs cannot produce, so applications are declined; and (ii) *high cost-to-serve/collateral requirements*: small loans are unprofitable to underwrite manually, and entrepreneurs without property cannot meet collateral demands. P2P platforms address these by using alternative data (bank transaction flows, e-payment records, marketplace sales) for automated credit scoring, which lowers assessment cost and serves thin-file firms, and by issuing unsecured notes sized to small needs, removing the collateral barrier.

**2.** *Donation-based*: backers receive nothing material — for example, funding a community flood-relief appeal. *Reward-based*: backers receive a product or token — for example, pre-ordering a Malaysian board game before production. *Debt-based (P2P)*: backers receive repayment with returns — for example, an investment note financing a Selangor food manufacturer's working capital. *Equity-based (ECF)*: backers receive shares — for example, buying equity in a Kuala Lumpur tech startup on a registered platform. Debt and equity types are regulated by the SC because they involve securities.

**3.** (1) *Application*: the SME applies online with SSM records, bank statements, financials and funding purpose. (2) *Credit assessment*: the platform verifies documents, analyses traditional and alternative data, and assigns a risk grade. (3) *Listing*: the note is published with amount, tenor, rate and purpose. (4) *Funding*: investors subscribe until the target is met; otherwise the campaign lapses. (5) *Disbursement*: the platform deducts fees and releases net proceeds. (6) *Repayment*: the issuer pays instalments or a bullet payment at maturity, which the platform distributes to investors.

**4.** The headline rate is paid on the face amount, but fees reduce the cash the issuer actually receives. For example, on a RM100,000 one-year note at 10% with a 2% origination fee, the issuer pays RM10,000 in returns plus a RM2,000 fee (RM12,000 total) while receiving only RM98,000. The effective annual cost is RM12,000 ÷ RM98,000 ≈ 12.2%, which is higher than the headline 10%. Servicing or late fees would raise the effective cost further.

**5.** Digital financing widens access to capital for MSMEs excluded from bank credit, but exposes retail investors to default, platform failure and fraud. Overly lax rules invite scandals that destroy trust; overly strict rules would strangle a channel that serves borrowers banks ignore. Malaysia balances the two by allowing the activity inside a regulated perimeter: the SC registers platforms as Recognized Market Operators, requires due diligence and disclosure, and caps retail investment amounts, while sophisticated investors enjoy wider access (SC, 2024; Zetzsche et al., 2017). This permits innovation while containing the harm any single failure can inflict.

## Problem-Solving Corner

### Problem 1: Effective Annual Cost of a Six-Month Investment Note

Sri Cahaya Food Products Sdn. Bhd. issues a **RM150,000** investment note with a **six-month tenor** at **10% per annum (simple)**, repayable as a single bullet payment at maturity. The platform charges a **2% origination fee**, deducted from the proceeds. There are no other fees.

*Assumptions: simple (not compounded) returns; bullet repayment at month six; fee deducted upfront.*

**Solution**

**Step 1 — Returns payable to investors.**
Returns = RM150,000 × 10% × (6/12) = **RM7,500**

**Step 2 — Origination fee.**
Fee = RM150,000 × 2% = **RM3,000**

**Step 3 — Net cash received by the issuer.**
Net proceeds = RM150,000 − RM3,000 = **RM147,000**

**Step 4 — Amount repaid at maturity.**
Repayment = RM150,000 + RM7,500 = **RM157,500**

**Step 5 — Total financing cost and six-month cost rate.**
Total cost = RM7,500 + RM3,000 = **RM10,500**
Six-month cost = RM10,500 ÷ RM147,000 = **7.14%** (per six months)

**Step 6 — Annualise.**
Simple annualisation: 7.14% × 2 = **14.29% per annum**
Effective annual rate (compounded): (1 + 0.0714)² − 1 = **14.80% per annum**

**Interpretation.** Although the note's headline rate is 10% per annum, the issuer's effective annual cost is about **14.3%–14.8%**, because the fee raises the cost and the fee deduction shrinks the usable funds. If the platform also charged a monthly servicing fee, the effective cost would be higher still.

### Problem 2: Investor Dilution in an Equity Crowdfunding Campaign

TechSihat Sdn. Bhd., a health-tech startup in Kuala Lumpur, has **1,000,000 ordinary shares** outstanding, all held by its two founders. The company raises **RM500,000** through an ECF campaign at an agreed **pre-money valuation of RM2,000,000**.

**(a)** Compute the price per share, the number of new shares issued, and the founders' ownership after the campaign.

**(b)** Suppose the founders had accepted a pre-money valuation of RM1,500,000 instead. How does their ownership change?

**(c)** Does the dilution in part (a) necessarily make the founders worse off? Explain.

**Solution**

**(a)** Price per share = pre-money valuation ÷ existing shares = RM2,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = **RM2.00**.
New shares issued = amount raised ÷ price per share = RM500,000 ÷ RM2.00 = **250,000 shares**.
Post-money valuation = RM2,000,000 + RM500,000 = **RM2,500,000**; total shares = 1,250,000.
Investors' stake = 250,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = **20%**; founders' stake = **80%** — dilution of 20 percentage points.

**(b)** Price per share = RM1,500,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = RM1.50. New shares = RM500,000 ÷ RM1.50 ≈ **333,333 shares**; total ≈ 1,333,333.
Investors' stake = RM500,000 ÷ RM2,000,000 post-money = **25%**; founders' stake falls to **75%**. A lower pre-money valuation means the founders give up more ownership for the same money.

**(c)** Not necessarily. Before the round, the founders owned 100% of a company worth RM2,000,000 (RM2,000,000 in value). After the round, they own 80% of a company worth RM2,500,000 — also RM2,000,000 on paper, *plus* the company now holds RM500,000 in cash to fund growth. If the money raises the company's value over time, the founders' smaller percentage of a larger company can be worth far more than 100% of a stagnant one. Dilution reduces *percentage ownership*, not automatically *value*.

### Problem 3: Diversification and Default Risk for a P2P Investor

Pn. Lim, a retail investor, places **RM10,000** into P2P notes. Consider two strategies over six months. Every note pays 10% per annum (simple) with bullet repayment; one issuer out of every twenty defaults, paying nothing during the tenor, and investors eventually recover **40% of principal** from the defaulted issuer's assets. Ignore platform fees and taxes.

*Strategy A:* all RM10,000 in one note.
*Strategy B:* RM500 into each of 20 notes.

**Solution**

**Strategy A (concentrated).** If the single note is the one that defaults: recovery = 40% × RM10,000 = RM4,000. Net outcome = **−RM6,000 (−60%)** in six months. If it does not default, the gain is RM10,000 × 5% = RM500 (+5%). The outcome is binary — large loss or modest gain.

**Strategy B (diversified).** Nineteen notes perform; one defaults.
- Principal returned by performing notes: 19 × RM500 = RM9,500
- Returns from performing notes: 19 × (RM500 × 10% × 6/12) = 19 × RM25 = **RM475**
- Recovery from the defaulted note: 40% × RM500 = **RM200**
- Total received = RM9,500 + RM475 + RM200 = **RM10,175**
- Net gain = **RM175**, i.e. about **1.75% over six months (≈3.5% annualised)**

**Interpretation.** With diversification, a single default is an expected cost that returns from the other nineteen notes largely absorb; the portfolio still earns a profit. Without diversification, the same default event destroys 60% of the capital. This is why spreading small amounts across many notes — and respecting the retail investment limits in the *Guidelines on Recognized Markets* — is fundamental risk management for P2P investors. It also shows that a portfolio's *expected* return is always lower than the headline note rate once realistic defaults are priced in.
